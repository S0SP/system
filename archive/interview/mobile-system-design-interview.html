Who this is for

If you’re a mobile engineer (iOS, Android, Flutter, React Native, etc.) and you need to:

I’m assuming you already know how to develop mobile apps. You understand the basics of networking, data persistence, and why you shouldn’t do heavy work on the main thread. You’ve shipped something to production and dealt with the consequences.

What you might not have is a structured way to think about system design. That’s what we’re covering here…

Interview Structure

Let’s talk about what actually happens in a mobile system design interview:

The format can vary wildly between companies. Some give you 45 minutes, others give you 60. Some want you on a whiteboard, others use tools like CodeSignal or HackerRank. Some companies even ask backend system design questions in mobile interviews, which is a separate discussion entirely.

But most mobile-focused interviews follow a similar arc.

Here’s what you can typically expect…

Typical flow

Most interviews break down roughly like this:

At a high level, the flow is:

  • Requirements and scope (5-10 minutes): clarify what you’re designing and the constraints

  • API and data needs (5-10 minutes): define what the app needs from the server

  • Architecture (10-15 minutes): outline client components and data flow

  • Deep dive (15-20 minutes): walk one critical flow or adjust for a new constraint

  • Wrap-up (1-5 minutes): summarize decisions and tradeoffs

I’ll go deep on how to execute each phase in the next section…

What’s expected at different levels

Interviewers don’t judge all mobile engineers the same way.

Here’s what interviewers are actually looking for based on your level:

Junior to mid-level engineers

You’re expected to:

  • Ask clarifying questions (even if they seem obvious)

  • Draw a reasonable architecture diagram without major gaps

  • Explain common mobile patterns (caching, background tasks, handling lifecycle)

  • Discuss basic tradeoffs - ”This approach is simpler but uses more memory.”

You’re not expected to:

  • Know every edge case off the top of your head

  • Have opinions on monorepo versus multi-repo

  • Design for 100 million users on day one

If you get stuck on something complex (like offline conflict resolution), just say so. “I haven’t built a system that merges offline edits before, but I’d start with last-write-wins and ask about requirements,” is fine.

Honesty beats bullshitting.

Senior engineers

Everything from junior/mid, plus:

  • Proactively uncover hidden requirements without prompting - ”What about users on limited data plans?”

  • Propose architecture with clear reasoning for your choices - ”I’m using a repository pattern here because we need to support offline-first”

  • Demonstrate deep knowledge of mobile-specific challenges (app lifecycle, memory pressure, battery drain, offline sync)

  • Adapt when requirements change mid-interview - ”Okay, if we need to support 50MB video uploads, we’ll need background upload with chunking…”

You should drive the conversation.

The interviewer shouldn’t have to pull answers out of you.

Staff and principal engineers

Everything from senior, plus:

  • Frame the problem: “Before we design this, let’s talk about whether we even need offline support for this use case.”

  • Consider team structure: “If we structure this as a separate module, we can have one team own it independently.”

  • Think long-term: “This works for v1, but if we add feature X later, we’ll need to refactor. Let’s design for that now.”

  • Make architectural tradeoffs at the system level: modularization strategy, build times, deployment, backward compatibility

You’re expected to think like a tech lead or architect, not just an IC implementing a feature. The interviewer wants to see if you can make decisions that affect the entire team or org.

Now that you know what the interview structure looks like, let’s talk about the actual process you’ll use during those 45 minutes.

Core Process

The interview structure gives you the timeline. But what do you actually do in each phase?

This is the process you’ll follow, roughly in order, during the first 30-40 minutes of the interview. Think of it as a checklist for making sure you forget nothing critical.

You won’t always do these steps in exactly this order. Sometimes you’ll jump back and forth. Sometimes the interviewer will steer you in a different direction. That’s fine.

But this is the general flow that works:

A reusable pattern (applies to every step)

Most steps look different, but the rhythm is the same:

  • Ask 2-3 high-impact questions before designing.

  • Restate what you heard and get a clear “yes.”

  • Write down decisions: constraints, must-haves versus nice-to-haves, and open questions.

  • Call out red flags early instead of waiting.

  • You don’t have to solve everything on the spot. Show you see the issue, pick a reasonable tradeoff, and move on.

Capturing the briefing

Here’s how most system design interviews start:

The interviewer says something like, “Design a feed for a social media app,” or “Build a way for users to share photos with friends.”

And your brain immediately starts racing: Should I ask about the backend? Should I start drawing? What if I forget to mention caching?

Before you do any of that, pause.

Your first job is not to design anything. It’s understanding what you’re being asked to design.

This sounds obvious, but most candidates blow past this step in 60 seconds and then spend 40 minutes designing the wrong thing…

What you’re actually trying to do

In the first 5-10 minutes, you’re trying to turn a vague prompt into something concrete enough that you know what problem you’re solving.

You’re not trying to get every detail. You’re trying to get enough clarity that you’re not completely guessing.

Think of it like this: If someone asks you to “design a messaging feature,” do they want:

  • WhatsApp (end-to-end encrypted, real-time, requires phone number)

  • Slack (threaded, searchable, integrates with other tools)

  • Twitter/X DMs (simple, text-only, tied to social graph)

All of those are “messaging.” They’re also completely different architectures.

So your job is to ask questions that reveal which one they actually want.

Questions that actually matter

Here’s what you should ask:

About success criteria:

  • “What does success look like for this feature?” (Are we optimizing for speed? Reliability? Simplicity?)

  • “What’s the most important thing this needs to do well?” (If we could only do one thing right, what would it be?)

About scope:

  • “Is this the whole app, or are we focusing on one part?” (Don’t design all of Instagram if they just want the feed)

  • “What platforms are we targeting?” (iOS only? Android? Cross-platform?)

About constraints:

  • “What network conditions should we plan for?” (Always online? Intermittent? Fully offline?)

  • “Are there any technical constraints I should know about?” (Existing systems we need to integrate with? Legacy code?)

About users:

  • “Who are the primary users?” (Consumers? Enterprise? Internal tools?)

  • “What devices are they on?” (Flagship phones? Low-end Android? Tablets?)

Don’t ask everything.

Pick the questions that will most change your design. You have limited time.

A bad and a good example

Bad example:

Interviewer: “Design a feed for a social media app.”

You: “Okay, so I’ll use MVVM for the architecture. We’ll have a ViewModel that fetches data from a Repository, which talks to both a local database and a REST API. UI will be built with—”

Interviewer (internally): They didn’t ask a single question. Do they even know what they’re designing?

You just spent 3 minutes designing an architecture without knowing:

  • Whether it needs to work offline

  • Whether it’s a text-based feed or heavy on images and video

  • Whether it requires pre-caching for a smoother scrolling experience

That’s a red flag. Interviewers are explicitly looking for whether you ask questions.

Good example:

Interviewer: “Design a feed for a social media app.”

You: “Okay, let me make sure I understand what we’re designing. Is this feed mostly images and video, or mostly text? That affects caching and pre-fetching on mobile.”

Interviewer: “Mostly images, some video.”

You: “Got it. For mobile, I’d plan on disk caching images (and maybe short videos) and pre-fetching upcoming content so the feed loads fast and works offline. Is that aligned with what you want?”

Interviewer: “Yes, basic offline support for previously loaded content is important.”

You: “Makes sense. One more thing: do we need live updates, or is pull-to-refresh enough for v1?”

Interviewer: “Pull-to-refresh is fine.”

You: “Perfect. So, to summarize: we’re designing a media-heavy feed with basic offline support. We’ll cache images (and maybe short videos), pre-fetch upcoming content for fast loads, and start with pull-to-refresh for updates. Does that sound right?”

Interviewer: “Yep, that’s it.”

Briefing wrap-up (red flags + next steps)

Sometimes the briefing is incomplete or contradictory. Call it out early instead of guessing…

Common red flags:

  • Requirements that are vague or overly broad - ”make it fast.”

  • Contradictory statements - ”we want it simple, but also highly customizable.”

  • Missing critical info (no mention of offline, auth, or data sources)

When you spot these, call them out:

“You mentioned we want it to be ‘fast.’ Can you be more specific? Are we talking about load time under 1 second, smooth scrolling, or something else?”

“I’m noticing we haven’t talked about where the data comes from. Should I assume we’re pulling from a REST API, or is there a different data source?”

Don’t rush ahead, hoping it’ll become clear later. It won’t. You’ll end up designing something that doesn’t match what they wanted…

Next steps once the briefing is clear:

  • Write down key constraints in the shared doc or on the whiteboard (”Offline support required”, “100k users”, “Existing auth system”)

  • Mentally categorize what is a must-have versus a nice-to-have

  • Identify the biggest unknowns you’ll need to address

Lesson: Don’t start designing until you know what you’re designing.

This can feel slow, but it’s faster than designing the wrong thing…

Defining scope and requirements

Okay, so you’ve asked your questions, and you have a basic understanding of what you’re designing. Now comes the hard part: figuring out what you’re actually going to design.

Here’s the problem: If someone asks you to “design a messaging feature,” there are about 47 different things they could mean. Do they want read receipts? Typing indicators? Group chats? Voice messages? End-to-end encryption? Reactions? Threads?

You can’t fit all of that into v1. You probably can’t even fit half of it.

So your job is to separate what’s essential from what’s nice-to-have.

This is where most people mess up. They either:

  1. Try to include everything at once and run out of time

  2. Design the wrong things and miss what actually matters

  3. Don’t ask at all and just guess

All three are bad. Let’s talk about how to do this right:

What you’re trying to figure out

You need to answer these questions:

What’s critical for v1?

If you could only ship three things, what would they be? What’s the minimum that makes this feature work?

For a messaging app, maybe it’s: send text, receive text, see message history. That’s it. Everything else is optional.

What’s nice-to-have but not required?

These are the things that would be great to include, but aren’t deal breakers if you skip them.

Read receipts? Nice-to-have. Typing indicators? Nice-to-have. Reactions? Nice-to-have.

What are the dependencies?

Does this feature require something else to exist first? Authentication? A backend API? A local database?

If those don’t exist yet, you need to account for that in your design.

What are the non-functional requirements?

Performance targets (load time under 2 seconds), security requirements (encrypt messages), and accessibility (VoiceOver support).

Breaking down features to prioritize

Instead of prioritizing entire features, break them into pieces and prioritize those.

Instead of asking, “Do we need a messaging feature?”, ask:

“For messaging, do we need:

  • Sending text messages (yes)

  • Sending images (maybe)

  • Group chats (no, not for v1)

  • Read receipts (nice-to-have)

  • Message editing (no)

  • Message deletion (yes, for privacy reasons).”

Now you have a much clearer picture of what to design.

Example: Chat feature

  • Essential: Send and receive messages, see message history

  • Optional: Read receipts, typing indicators, retry mechanism for failed sends

Example: Payment feature

  • Essential: Main payment flow with Apple Pay and Google Pay

  • Optional: Third-party payment providers like PayPal, saving payment methods for later

Example: Multi-user calendar feature

  • Essential: Single-user calendar with built-in suggestions

  • Optional: Multi-user support (better to make it work for one person first, then add collaboration)

See the pattern? You’re being specific about what goes in v1 and what gets deferred.

Signals, red flags, and wrap-up

Bad example:

You: “Okay, so we need feature flags. I’ll design it to support everything: A/B testing, multivariate testing, gradual rollouts, user segmentation, analytics integration, real-time updates, flag dependencies, versioning…”

Interviewer (internally): We asked for basic feature flags. Why are they designing LaunchDarkly?

You’re trying to do too much at once. You’ll run out of time and never finish the design.

Good example:

You: “Okay, so for this feature flag system, what’s absolutely critical for v1?”

Interviewer: “We need to remotely enable or disable features without releasing a new app version.”

You: “Got it. What about more advanced features like gradual rollouts, A/B testing, or user targeting based on criteria like device type or location? Are those must-haves or can we defer them?”

Interviewer: “Simple on/off flags are the priority. Gradual rollouts would be nice-to-have, but A/B testing can wait.”

You: “And should the flags update in real-time, or is it okay if they refresh when the app restarts?”

Interviewer: “Real-time would be ideal, but we can start with refresh-on-launch if that’s simpler.”

You: “Perfect. So, to summarize: For v1, we need a system that can remotely toggle features on/off, with flag values updating when the app launches. Gradual rollouts are optional for v1, and A/B testing is deferred to v2. Does that sound right?”

Interviewer: “Yep, exactly.”

You thought about the scope, allowing you to ship earlier. Even if it means fewer features at the start.

Turning requirements into a system diagram

This is where you turn vague requirements into concrete types, then turn those types into a graph you can reason about.

A fast, reliable workflow

Step 1: Write requirements in plain language

  • Use phrases like “a way to” or “some sort of” to keep it flexible

  • Example: “A chat feature should have a way to fetch recent messages and a way to send messages.”

Step 2: Turn requirements into types

  • Give each requirement a name so you can talk about it

  • Example: MessageHistory, MessageSender, TypingIndicator, NotificationHandler

Step 3: Sketch the graph

  • Put the UI at the top, connect the types under it

  • Keep it rough. This is a sketch, not a final diagram

Step 4: Mark unknowns and decompose

  • Use dashed boxes for parts you haven’t figured out yet

  • Pick one unknown and break it down into smaller systems

Step 5: Group by domain when it grows

  • Group related parts into domains or modules

  • This keeps the diagram readable and maps to the code structure

Tiny example (requirements to types)

Requirements:

  • A way to fetch recent messages

  • A way to send a message

  • A way to send images

  • Some way to show typing status

  • Supports push notifications

Types:

  • MessageHistory

  • MessageSender

  • ImageSender

  • TypingIndicator

  • NotificationHandler

Now turn those types into a quick sketch. Mark unknowns however you like (dashed boxes, question marks, or notes).

Once you have the types, sketch how they connect and mark the unknowns. That graph becomes your architecture.

Keep breaking down vague types until you can explain the core data flow and responsibilities without guessing.

Finding secondary or hidden requirements

Once you have a first-pass diagram, pause and look for what’s missing.

The sketch will surface edge cases and implied requirements you haven’t asked about yet. This is where you go back and fill in those gaps.

This is still part of the requirements phase. You can do it right after the initial questions or after a first quick sketch. The goal is to surface what wasn’t said.

Next, requirements aren’t just what people tell you. They’re also what people assume you know, what they forgot to mention, and what they think is “obvious” but isn’t.

In interviews, this is intentional. The interviewer gives you a vague prompt and waits to see if you’ll uncover the hidden stuff. In jobs, it’s usually accidental, but the result is the same: you’re expected to figure out what wasn’t said.

This is where most candidates lose points.

They take the initial requirements at face value and start designing. Then halfway through, the interviewer asks, “What about offline support?” or “How would this work on a tablet?” and the candidate realizes they never thought about it.

Let me show you how to avoid that…

Listen for vague language

When someone says the feature should be “fast,” “smooth,” or “user-friendly,” what do they actually mean?

“Fast” could mean:

  • Loads in under 1 second

  • Scrolling at 60fps

  • No blocking the main thread

  • Instant feedback when the user taps something

“Smooth” could mean:

  • No jank during animations

  • Graceful degradation when the network is slow

  • Optimistic UI updates

“User-friendly” could mean:

  • Works without training

  • Accessible to screen readers

  • Handles errors without crashing

When you hear soft language, dig deeper:

“You mentioned it should feel ‘fast.’ Can you be more specific? Are we talking about initial load time, scroll performance, or something else?”

“When you say ‘seamless,’ do you mean the user shouldn’t see loading states, or that transitions should be animated, or both?”

Walk through the feature as a user

Imagine you’re actually using the feature. Tap by tap. Screen by screen. What happens?

Let’s say you’re designing a photo upload feature. Walk through it:

  1. User taps “upload photo”

  2. What happens next? Do they pick from the gallery or take a new photo?

  3. User picks a photo

  4. Does it upload immediately? Do they edit it first? Can they add a caption?

  5. User hits “upload”

  6. What if the file is 50MB? Do we compress it? Show progress? What if they close the app mid-upload?

  7. Upload completes

  8. How do they know it worked? Toast? Push notification? Does the photo appear in their feed immediately?

See how many questions pop up just by walking through the flow? Most of these won’t be mentioned in the initial briefing. You have to ask...

Good questions to surface hidden requirements:

“You mentioned users can upload photos. Can they edit or crop them first, or do we upload as-is?”

“If the upload fails midway, do we retry automatically, or does the user have to start over?”

“Should upload continue in the background if the user closes the app?”

Platform and device considerations

Mobile isn’t monolithic. There are differences that matter.

Questions about platforms:

  • “What OS versions do we need to support?” (iOS 16+? Affects what APIs you can use)

  • “Are we targeting emerging markets?” (If yes, you need to care about app size, low-end devices, and data costs)

  • “Do we need to support tablets or other formats, such as foldable devices?” (Different screen sizes change your layout strategy)

Why this matters in interviews:

If you’re designing for users in markets with expensive data plans, you can’t just download 10MB of images on every feed refresh. You need to talk about compression, caching, and incremental loading.

Interviewers want to see if you think about these things without being prompted.

Scalability and performance

Even if the interviewer doesn’t mention scale, ask about it.

“How many users are we expecting? Hundreds? Thousands? Millions?”

“What’s the data volume we’re dealing with? Are we showing 10 items or 10,000?”

“Where are users located? Single region or global?”

Why? Because the answers change your design:

  • 1,000 users: Simple polling might be fine

  • 1,000,000 users: You need to think about caching, pagination strategy, and bandwidth tradeoffs

  • Global users: You need to think about latency, regional API endpoints, and offline support

Signals and checklist

Bad example:

Interviewer: “Design a system for users to upload and share photos.”

You: “Okay, so users tap a button, pick a photo, and we upload it to the server. Done.”

Interviewer: “What if the upload fails?”

You: “Oh, um, we’d show an error message.”

Interviewer: “What if the user closes the app during upload?”

You: “Uh… I guess the upload would cancel?”

Interviewer (internally): They didn’t think about any of this.

You took the prompt at face value and didn’t dig deeper. The interviewer had to pull every edge case out of you.

Good example:

Interviewer: “Design a system for users to upload and share photos.”

You: “Got it. A few clarifying questions: When you say ‘upload,’ are we assuming users can edit or crop photos before uploading, or do we upload the original file as-is?”

Interviewer: “Good question. Let’s keep it simple for v1, no editing.”

You: “Makes sense. And if an upload is taking a while, should it continue in the background if the user closes the app or navigates away?”

Interviewer: “Yes, background upload would be important.”

You: “Okay. One more thing: are we targeting users in specific regions, or is this global? I’m asking because it affects how we think about bandwidth and image compression.”

Interviewer: “Assume global, including emerging markets with limited data.”

You: “Perfect. So I’m thinking we’ll need aggressive image compression, background upload support, and probably some retry logic for flaky connections. Does that sound right?”

Interviewer: “Exactly what I was hoping you’d think about.”

This time, you didn’t take the functionality at face value; you thought deeply about the problem. By asking questions, you show the interviewer you’re uncovering the deeper requirements.

A quick checklist of hidden requirements:

Even if the interviewer doesn’t mention these, it’s worth calling them out:

  • Data size limits (payload size, file size)

  • Offline expectations (read-only cache versus full sync)

  • Platform constraints (OS versions, device classes)

  • Compliance or analytics requirements

Uncovering failure requirements and edge cases

Mobile apps live in a volatile environment…

Users close apps mid-operation. Networks drop. Devices run out of battery. Storage fills up. Apps get killed by the OS for using too much memory.

If you design for only the happy path, your app will break in production. Guaranteed.

In interviews, this is where you separate yourself from web engineers. Backend services can assume reliable networks, persistent connections, and predictable resources. Mobile apps can’t. Showing you understand this tells the interviewer you’ve built real mobile apps.

Let me show you what to think about:

Network and offline handling

This is non-negotiable for mobile. Even if the interviewer doesn’t mention offline support, you should bring it up.

Questions to ask:

“What happens when the user loses network mid-operation? Do we queue the action for later, or fail immediately?”

“If a user makes changes while offline, and those same changes happen on another device while they’re offline, how do we resolve the conflict?”

“Should we show stale data from the cache, or a loading state, or an error when we can’t reach the server?”

Why these questions matter:

If you’re designing a messaging app and the user sends a message while offline, what happens?

  • Option 1: Show it immediately in the UI (optimistic update), queue it, and sync when the network returns

  • Option 2: Disable the send button until the network is available

  • Option 3: Let them send it, but show a “pending” state

Each approach has tradeoffs. The interviewer wants to see whether you’ll think them through.

Conflict resolution strategies

If you mentioned offline support, the interviewer might ask: “What if the same message is edited on two devices while both are offline?”

You need a strategy…

Last-write-wins:

Simplest approach. Whoever syncs last overwrites the others. Works for some use cases (updating profile pictures), terrible for others (collaborative editing).

Manual resolution:

Show the user both versions and let them pick. Works for high-value data (documents), annoying for trivial stuff (todo list items).

Operational transforms or CRDTs:

Automatically merge changes. Complex to implement, but gives the best user experience. Probably overkill for most mobile interview scenarios.

What to say in an interview:

“For this use case, I’d go with last-write-wins for simplicity. If we need more sophisticated conflict resolution later, we could add it, but it’s probably not critical for v1.”

You don’t need to implement CRDTs (Conflict-free Replicated Data Types) on a whiteboard. You just need to show that you know the problem exists and have a reasonable approach.

Retry mechanisms and degraded experiences

Mobile networks are unreliable. Requests fail. Timeouts happen.

Questions to ask:

“If a network request fails, should we retry automatically? How many times?”

“Should we use exponential back-off for retries, or retry immediately?”

“What happens if we’ve retried 3 times, and it’s still failing? Do we give up, or queue it for later?”

Why this matters:

If you’re uploading a photo and the request times out, you don’t want to re-upload the entire 10MB file immediately. That wastes the user’s data and battery.

Better approach:

  • Retry with exponential back-off (1s, 2s, 4s, 8s)

  • If still failing, queue it and retry when conditions improve (Wi-Fi available, battery not low)

  • Show the user that the upload is pending so they know what’s happening

Example of good thinking:

“For photo uploads, I’d implement a retry queue. If the initial upload fails, we’ll retry with exponential back-off. After 3 retries, we’ll queue it and wait for better network conditions. We’ll show a ‘pending upload’ indicator so the user knows it’s not lost.”

What happens when the app dies?

Mobile apps don’t run continuously like web servers. The OS can kill your app at any time to reclaim memory. Users can background your app mid-operation.

Questions to ask:

“If the user backgrounds the app while uploading a file, should the upload continue or pause?”

“If the app is killed by the OS mid-sync, how do we resume when the user reopens the app?”

“What state do we need to persist to disk so we can recover after an app restart?”

Why this matters:

If a user starts uploading a 50MB video, backgrounds the app to check a text message, and iOS kills your app to free memory, what happens to that upload?

  • Bad: If you didn’t implement background upload, it cancels. The user has to start over.

  • Good: If you implemented background upload with state persistence, it resumes where it left off.

What to say in the interview:

“We’d need to implement background uploads for large files. On iOS, that means using URLSession with a background configuration. We’d persist the upload state to disk so if the app is killed and reopened, we can resume the upload instead of starting over.”

Shows you know mobile-specific APIs and why they exist.

Mobile-specific constraints

These are the things that separate mobile from backend…

Battery consumption:

  • Should we batch network requests instead of making them immediately?

  • Should we skip background sync if the device is in low-power mode?

  • Should we reduce upload quality to save battery?

Metered data:

  • Should we warn users before they download large files over a cellular network?

  • Should we skip automatic sync on limited data plans?

  • Should we compress data more aggressively to save bandwidth?

Memory limitations:

  • Can we load all 10,000 feed items into memory, or do we need pagination?

  • Should we cache images on disk rather than in memory?

  • What’s our eviction policy when we hit memory pressure?

Storage constraints:

  • How much disk space can we use for caching?

  • What happens when the device runs out of storage?

  • Should we let users configure cache size limits?

App size optimization:

  • If we’re targeting emerging markets, can we keep the app under 50MB?

  • Should we download assets on-demand instead of bundling them?

Questions to ask in the interview:

“If users are on limited data plans, should we warn them before downloading large files?”

“What’s our strategy when memory pressure is high? Do we aggressively clear caches?”

Why bringing these up matters:

Most candidates forget about these constraints. If you mention them, you immediately signal that you’ve dealt with real production mobile apps.

What bad and good looks like

Bad example:

Interviewer: “Design a photo-sharing app.”

You: “Okay, users tap a button, pick a photo, we upload it to S3, and display it in the feed.”

Interviewer: “What happens if the upload fails?”

You: “Um… we’d show an error message?”

Interviewer: “What if the app crashes mid-upload?”

You: “Uh… they’d have to try again?”

Interviewer: “What about memory? Can you load 1000 high-res images?”

You: “I guess we’d… compress them?”

You’re being reactive instead of proactive. The interviewer has to pull every edge case out of you. That’s a red flag.

Good example:

Interviewer: “Design a photo-sharing app.”

You: “Got it. A few questions about failure scenarios: What should happen if the user starts uploading a photo and then loses network connection?”

Interviewer: “Good question. What would you recommend?”

You: “I’d queue the upload and retry when network returns. We’d show a ‘pending upload’ indicator, so the user knows it’s not lost. For large photos, we’d use background upload, so it continues even if the app is backgrounded.”

Interviewer: “What if they edit the same photo on two devices while offline?”

You: “For a photo-sharing app, I’d use last-write-wins. Whichever device syncs first sets the photo. If they edit it again on another device, that overwrites it. It’s simple and works for this use case. If we needed more sophisticated conflict resolution, we could add it later.”

Interviewer: “Good. What about memory constraints?”

You: “For the feed, we’d use pagination and only keep visible images in memory. We’d cache downloaded images on disk and use an LRU (least-recently used) policy to evict old images when we hit our cache size limit. Probably cap it at 100MB or let users configure it.”

This shows you’re thinking about the problem beyond what’s asked of you.

Uncovering backend requirements

Your mobile app doesn’t exist in isolation. It talks to servers. It needs APIs. It needs data.

In a system design interview, you’re not designing the entire backend (that’s a different interview). But you are responsible for defining what your mobile app needs from the backend.

This is where you show you understand the boundary between the client and the server and the constraints mobile apps impose on API design.

What APIs does the mobile app need?

Start by listing out the obvious endpoints…

For a feed app:

  • GET /feed - fetch posts

  • POST /posts/:id/like - like a post

  • GET /posts/:id - get a specific post

For a messaging app:

  • GET /conversations - list conversations

  • POST /messages - send a message

  • GET conversations/:id/messages - get messages for a conversation

Simple enough. But there’s more to think about.

“What data do we actually need in each response? Can we optimize the payload size?”

“Should we have separate endpoints for different use cases, or one flexible endpoint?”

“Do we need different API versions for iOS versus Android, or can they share the same API?”

Protocol selection

Most mobile apps use REST. It’s simple, well-understood, and works fine for most use cases.

But sometimes you need something different.

REST:

  • Simple, stateless, well-documented

  • Works great for CRUD operations

  • Can be chatty if you need multiple round-trips

  • Good default choice for most mobile apps

GraphQL:

  • Flexible: clients request exactly what they need

  • Reduces over-fetching and under-fetching

  • Single endpoint instead of many

  • Adds complexity to both client and server

  • Good if you have many different clients with different data needs

WebSockets:

  • Bidirectional, persistent connection

  • Great for real-time updates (chat, live data)

  • More complex to manage (reconnection, state)

  • Uses more battery than polling if poorly implemented

  • Good for features that need instant updates

What to say in the interview:

“For this feed feature, I’d use REST. It’s simple and fits the request/response model well. If we needed real-time updates later, we could add WebSockets, but REST is fine for v1.”

You’re making a reasoned choice with clear tradeoffs.

Pagination strategies

If you’re fetching a list of anything (posts, messages, users), you need pagination. You can’t load 10,000 items at once.

Offset-based pagination:

  • Request page 1: GET /posts?offset=0&limit=20

  • Request page 2: GET /posts?offset=20&limit=20

  • Simple to implement

  • Problem: If items are added/deleted between requests, you get duplicates or gaps

  • Works fine for mostly static data

Cursor-based pagination:

  • Request page 1: GET /posts?limit=20

    • returns cursor=”abc123”

  • Request page 2: GET /posts?cursor=abc123&limit=20

  • Avoids most duplicates/gaps if you use a stable sort; new items may still require a refresh

  • Works well for real-time feeds

  • Slightly more complex to implement

Keyset-based pagination:

  • Request posts after ID 100: GET /posts?after_id=100&limit=20

  • Similar to cursor-based, but uses actual IDs

  • Efficient for databases with indexed columns

  • Good for sorted data (by timestamp, ID)

What to say in the interview:

“For the feed, I’d use cursor-based pagination. Users can scroll through their feed, and new posts might be added as they scroll. Cursor-based ensures they don’t see duplicates or miss posts. We’d return a cursor in each API response that the client uses for the next page.”

Mobile-specific authentication considerations

Authentication on mobile differs from that on the web, and it comes up often because it affects storage, lifecycle, and background refresh.

Token storage:

On the web, you might use cookies or localStorage. On mobile:

  • Store tokens in the platform’s secure storage (encrypted, access-controlled)

  • Never store tokens in plain text in UserDefaults/SharedPreferences

Token refresh:

Tokens expire. On mobile, you need to handle refresh without forcing the user to log in again.

Strategy:

  • Store both access token (short-lived, 1 hour) and a refresh token (long-lived, 30 days)

  • When access token expires, use a refresh token to get a new access token

  • If the refresh token expires, the user has to log in again

  • Refresh proactively before expiration, not after a 401 response

Biometric authentication:

Users expect to use Face ID or Touch ID instead of typing passwords.

Implementation:

  • First login: user enters their password, you store tokens in secure storage with biometric protection

  • Subsequent logins: user authenticates with biometrics, and you retrieve tokens from secure storage

  • Tokens aren’t stored outside secure storage; they’re read into memory only when needed

Handling auth state across app lifecycle:

What happens when the app backgrounds or dies?

  • If the app backgrounds for 5 minutes, tokens should still be valid

  • If the app is killed and reopened: retrieve tokens from secure storage, check if still valid

  • If tokens expired: try to refresh the token first, only requiring login if that fails

Questions to ask in the interview:

“Where should we store auth tokens? I’m thinking platform secure storage since it’s encrypted and survives app restarts.”

“Should we support biometric authentication, or just password login?”

“How long should tokens be valid? I’d suggest short-lived access tokens with refresh tokens so users don’t have to log in every hour.”

Bad versus good example

Bad example:

Interviewer: “What APIs do you need?”

You: “We’ll call the backend to get data.”

Interviewer: “What endpoints specifically?”

You: “Um… probably a GET endpoint for the feed?”

Interviewer: “How do you handle pagination?”

You: “We’d… load more when they scroll?”

Interviewer: “What about authentication?”

You: “Store the token somewhere?”

Vague, hand-wavy, no depth. The interviewer will conclude you haven’t thought about API design before.

Good example:

You: “For the API, we’ll need a few endpoints: GET feed for fetching posts, POST /posts/:id/like for liking, and GET /user/:id for profiles. We’ll use cursor-based pagination for the feed since users might scroll through hundreds of posts.”

Interviewer: “What about real-time updates?”

You: “For v1, I’d use polling. We’ll poll for new posts every 60 seconds when the app is in the foreground. It’s simple and works fine for a feed. If we need instant updates later, we could add WebSockets, but polling is good enough to start.”

Interviewer: “How do you handle authentication?”

You: “We’d use token-based auth. User logs in once, we get an access token and refresh token from the server. Access tokens expire after 1 hour; refresh tokens expire after 30 days. We store both in secure storage. When the access token expires, we use the refresh token to get a new one. We’d also support Face ID so users don’t have to type their password every time.”

Now you’re going through the process to uncover the endpoints and token strategy that you need.

Practice problems

The best way to get better at system design is to practice. A lot.

  • Design a todo list app with offline support

  • Design a settings screen with user preferences

Intermediate level:

Closing Thoughts

Mobile system design interviews are intimidating. You’re given a vague prompt and 45 minutes to prove you know what you’re doing.

But they don’t have to be mysterious…

What we covered

This newsletter walked through the entire process:

  1. Capturing the briefing: Ask questions, clarify scope, confirm understanding

  2. Defining scope: Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves, break features into pieces

  3. Finding hidden requirements: Uncover what wasn’t said, think about edge cases, consider mobile constraints

  4. System diagram: Turn requirements into types, then into a graph you can reason about

  5. Failure scenarios: Plan for network loss, offline sync, app lifecycle, mobile-specific constraints

  6. Backend requirements: Define APIs, choose protocols, design for mobile bandwidth, and battery

  7. Storage strategy: Pick the right storage for each use case, implement caching, and handle security

  8. Avoid pitfalls: Start simple, explain your reasoning, and think about edge cases proactively

This is the same process whether you’re in an interview or planning a real feature at work. The skills transfer.

Takeaway

Most mobile engineers fail system design interviews because they jump straight into implementation without understanding the problem.

They start drawing boxes and arrows before they know what they’re designing.

The fix is simple: Spend the first 10-15 minutes asking questions and clarifying requirements. Get crystal clear on what you’re designing before you start designing.

Everything else follows from that.

What to do next

If you have an interview coming up:

  1. Practice the process on 3-5 different prompts

  2. Time yourself to 45 minutes

  3. Focus on asking good questions and uncovering hidden requirements

  4. Draw diagrams even if you’re just practicing alone (muscle memory matters)

  5. Explain your reasoning out loud as if you’re talking to an interviewer

If you want to get better at mobile architecture in general:

  1. Read the full book bundle for deeper examples and code-level details

  2. Study well-designed open source apps to see how they handle architecture

  3. Apply this process to features you’re shipping at work

  4. Review your past designs and ask: what would I do differently with this framework?

Either way, the process is the same. Understand the problem. Make it concrete. Show your work.
