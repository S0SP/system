Three Approaches to Accepting Payments

Before diving into the design, it’s worth understanding the landscape…

When a company needs to accept payments, there are three broad approaches, and choosing the wrong one for your scale wastes either years of engineering time or millions in unnecessary fees.

Option 1: Build your own payment processor

This means connecting directly to card networks (Visa, Mastercard), acquiring banking licenses, handling PCI DSS1 (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, which is the set of security requirements every company that touches card data must comply with), Level 1 compliance2, building your own fraud detection, and managing relationships with issuing banks.

The upside is lower transaction costs.

At a massive scale, even saving 0.5% off each transaction adds up to hundreds of millions per year. Amazon, Uber, and Airbnb have all moved in this direction for parts of their payment stack.

The downside is the cost to get there: acquiring necessary licenses alone takes 12-24 months, costs millions, and comes with ongoing regulatory obligations. So this approach only makes sense once you are processing billions in annual payment volume.

For anyone else, it is premature optimization at its extreme…

Option 2: Use a payment service provider (PSP) like Stripe, PayPal, Adyen, or Braintree

A PSP is a company that handles the entire payment processing chain on your behalf: card network integrations, banking relationships, fraud tooling, PCI DSS compliance, and global payment method support.

You pay a per-transaction fee, typically around 2.9% + $0.30 with Stripe, in exchange for not having to build or maintain any of that infrastructure. This is the right approach for most companies, from early-stage startups to large-scale platforms.

Even Shopify, which processes billions in payments annually, built its own payment product (Shopify Payments) on top of Stripe’s infrastructure rather than connecting directly to card networks.

Option 3: Use a no-code payment flow, such as Stripe Checkout

Stripe offers hosted checkout pages and payment links that require almost no backend integration.

You create a checkout session, redirect the user to Stripe’s hosted page, and Stripe handles everything: UI, payment method selection, and confirmation.

This is the fastest way to accept payments, and it works well for simple use cases: selling a single product, collecting donations, or building a quick prototype.

The trade-off is limited customization and less control over the payment experience.

You cannot deeply embed it in your own UI, easily build custom subscription logic, or react to payment events in real time with full control over the flow.

How Money Moves Through Card Network

Understanding the payment flow end-to-end is a prerequisite to designing a backend that handles it correctly…

Six entities participate in each card transaction:

  1. Cardholder

  2. Merchant (Business or individual selling the product or service)

  3. PSP (Payment Service Provider, in this case, Stripe)

  4. Acquiring bank (Merchant’s bank)

  5. Card network (Visa/Mastercard)

  6. Issuing bank (Cardholder’s bank)

Money flows through 3 distinct phases, each with different timing and failure modes…

Phase 1: Authorization (1-3 seconds)

The customer submits card details through Stripe Elements.

These are technically iframes hosted by Stripe, even if they look like part of your site. We use Stripe.js, which is a JavaScript library that Stripe requires you to load directly from their servers (for security reasons).

The process starts on the backend, where a PaymentIntent3 gets created via Stripe’s API to track the transaction lifecycle. This returns a client_secret to the frontend.

The customer then enters their card details into Stripe Elements - secure iframes hosted by Stripe that look like part of your site. Using Stripe.js, the client-side code tokenizes the card details so the PAN (Primary Account Number: a 14 to 16-digit number on a credit or debit card) never reaches the merchant’s server.

What is PaymentIntent?

A PaymentIntent is a stateful object Stripe uses to track a single payment from start to finish.

Think of it as the source of truth for a transaction…

In older systems, you’d just send a “charge” request and hope it worked. With modern payments, things are more complex because of 2FA (e.g., 3D Secure4) or fraud checks.

PaymentIntent manages this by moving through different states:

  • Requires payment method: You created the intent, but the user hasn’t typed their card yet.

  • Requires action: User must verify the payment in their bank app.

  • Processing: Stripe is talking to the banks.

  • Succeeded: Money is authorized or captured.

Stripe formats an ISO 8583 authorization message5 and sends it to the acquiring bank6, which identifies the card network via the BIN7 (first 6-8 digits) and forwards the request.

The card network routes it to the issuing bank, which checks card validity, available funds, CVV/AVS8 match, and fraud risk models.

  • The issuer returns a two-digit response code, approve or decline, back through the entire chain.

  • If approved, a hold gets placed on the cardholder’s available balance. But no money moves yet.

Mastercard reports an average network response time of 130 milliseconds; the full round-trip, including all hops, completes in 1-3 seconds.

Visa’s network handles a peak of 56,000+ messages per second.

Phase 2: Capture (immediate/delayed)

Capture9 is when the merchant tells acquiring bank (the merchant’s bank) to finalize the authorized amount.

For digital goods and subscriptions, capture occurs immediately. Stripe’s default is capture_method: automatic.

For physical goods, hotels, or ride-hailing, capture is delayed until fulfillment. You can capture an amount less than or equal to what was authorized, but never more. Authorization holds10 typically last 5 to 10 days.

Visa allows 10 days for standard e-commerce and up to 31 days for lodging.

Here’s the Engineering Risk:

If you don’t capture within 7 days, the issuing bank might cancel the hold. This is a common source of bugs. Your state machine must treat AUTHORIZED and CAPTURED as “distinct” states. If your system thinks a payment is authorized but the bank has dropped the hold, the capture call will fail.

So you need a background job to detect these “stuck” payments and mark them as expired.

Phase 3: Clearing and settlement (T+1 to T+3)

At the end of the business day, captured transactions get batched and sent to the card network for clearing.

The network calculates fees and exchanges transaction files overnight…

Settlement is when the actual money moves. The issuing bank sends the funds (minus fees) to the card network, which then passes them to the acquiring bank.

The acquiring bank finally deposits them into the merchant’s account.

In banking, “T” stands for the Transaction Date. T+2 means the money arrives two business days after the transaction. Stripe’s US default is T+2, though new accounts usually have an initial hold of 7 to 14 days.

Fee Breakdown

On a $100 US online transaction, the 2.9% + $0.30 fee gets split three ways:

  • Interchange11 (~$2.05): This goes to the Issuing Bank (the customer’s bank). This is the largest cut. It covers the bank’s risk and pays for the customer’s credit card rewards.

  • Assessment (~$0.16): This goes to the Card Network (Visa or Mastercard) for using their rails.

  • Markup (~$0.70): This is what Stripe keeps for providing the API, security, and infrastructure.

Functional and Non-Functional Requirements

Functional requirements for a payment backend serving a mid-to-large platform fall into 6 categories:

  1. Customers must be able to make one-time payments and save payment methods for reuse.

  2. Merchants or the platform must be able to accept payments, with support for marketplace-style split payments if needed.

  3. Refunds, both full and partial, must flow through a dedicated refund state machine.

  4. System must handle subscriptions and recurring billing, including proration on plan changes, trial periods, and Stripe’s Smart Retries for failed renewal payments.

  5. Backend must process Stripe webhooks reliably, as webhooks are the source of truth for payment status.

  6. The entire payment lifecycle must be modeled as a finite-state machine with enforced transitions.

Non-functional requirements are where payment systems diverge sharply from typical backend services:

  • Exactly-once payment processing is the key requirement. A double charge on a $1,000 purchase means money taken from a person twice. True exactly-once delivery is impossible in distributed systems (Two Generals Problem12), so the practical implementation is at-least-once delivery combined with idempotent processing and reconciliation.

  • High availability targets are extreme. Stripe itself maintains 99.999% API uptime.

  • Idempotency must be enforced at every layer: client-to-backend, backend-to-Stripe, and webhook processing. Every retry must produce the same result as the original request.

  • Consistency over availability. Payment systems are one of the few domains where the CAP theorem should tilt decisively toward consistency. A stale read that shows an incorrect balance or a lost write that drops a payment is far worse than brief unavailability. This is why every major payment platform, including Shopify, Uber, and Airbnb, uses SQL databases with ACID guarantees for core payment data.

Scale estimates for a mid-to-large platform

A platform processing 100,000 payments per day generates roughly 500,000-1,000,000 webhook events daily (each payment triggers 5-10 events across creation, authorization, capture, and related objects).

Stripe’s default API rate limit is 100 requests/second per account, with individual endpoints limited to 25 requests/second; higher limits are available by arrangement.

Payment API latency should be under 5 seconds end-to-end, including the Stripe round-trip, with internal service-to-service calls under 100ms.

High-Level Architecture

The architecture splits into seven components, each with a distinct responsibility.

It isolates the synchronous customer-facing path from asynchronous processing and keeps the webhook ingestion pipeline decoupled from business logic.

1 Payment API Service

It’s the synchronous entry point.

It receives payment requests from checkout, validates input, checks idempotency keys13 against the database, creates or retrieves the payment record, and returns an immediate response to the client.

For Stripe integrations, this service creates a PaymentIntent and returns the client_secret to the frontend, which uses Stripe.js to complete the payment (including 3D Secure challenges).

The API service should never block on downstream processing. It creates the payment record, dispatches work, and returns.

2 Stripe Integration Layer

It abstracts all Stripe-specific API calls behind a uniform interface.

This adapter handles Stripe’s error types, maps them to internal error codes, attaches idempotency keys to every POST request, and manages timeouts. Shopify and Airbnb both use this pattern; Airbnb calls it the “PSP adapter,” which isolates provider-specific logic from the core payment domain.

If you ever need to support a second PSP (Adyen, Braintree), only this layer changes.

3 Payment Database

This is the system of record.

It stores the current state of every payment, idempotency keys, and the immutable audit log. The schema design is covered in the next section, but the critical principle is: payments table holds a mutable current state (optimized for queries), while the payment events table holds an append-only immutable log of every state change (optimized for audit, debugging, and reconciliation).

Both exist in the same SQL database and get updated within the same transaction.

4 Webhook Receiver

It’s a lightweight HTTP endpoint that does exactly three things: verify the Stripe signature (HMAC-SHA25614 using the Stripe-Signature header), store the raw event, and return 200 OK.

It must respond within seconds, since Stripe has an approximately 20-second timeout. All business logic happens asynchronously. The receiver enqueues the event onto the message queue for processing by workers.

5 Message Queue

It (Kafka, SQS, or RabbitMQ) decouples webhook receipt from processing and provides at-least-once delivery guarantees.

Uber uses Apache Kafka as the backbone of their payment platform’s async stream processing. The queue also supports the transactional outbox pattern15: when a payment state change is committed to the database, an outbox record is written in the same transaction, then relayed to the queue by a separate process.

6 Background Job Workers

They handle 4 categories of async work:

  • Webhook event processing: dequeuing events and applying state transitions idempotently,

  • Retry workers: retrying failed PSP calls with exponential backoff,

  • A reconciliation worker: daily comparison of internal records against Stripe’s records,

  • And a stuck-payment detector: alerting on payments in intermediate states beyond a configurable threshold.

7 Ledger

It provides double-entry bookkeeping16.

Every money movement is recorded as a balanced pair of debit and credit entries. Uber explicitly built its next-generation payment platform on double-entry bookkeeping for auditability, and Stripe’s own ledger system logs approximately 5 billion money-movement events daily.

The ledger is append-only.

If a mistake needs correction, a new reversing entry is inserted, never an update or delete.

The high-level architecture tells us what to build, but database design is where correctness is either enforced or lost. Every pattern we covered above, idempotency, exactly-once processing, the state machine, all of them depend on the database schema doing the right thing.

If we get this wrong, no amount of application-level logic will matter.

So let's see what the database design looks like…

Database Schema to Enforce Correctness

The database design must enforce three invariants: no duplicate payments (idempotency), no invalid state transitions (state machine), and a complete audit trail (event log).

Five tables form the core schema…

The payments table stores the current payment state:

Key design decisions:

  • Amounts stored as integers in the smallest currency unit (cents for USD) to avoid floating-point errors, matching Stripe’s own convention.

  • Version column enables optimistic locking17.

  • The idempotency_key has a unique constraint that serves as the first line of defense against duplicate payments.

  • Indexes on customer_id, merchant_id, stripe_payment_intent_id, and a partial index on status (excluding terminal states) optimize the most common query patterns.

The idempotency_keys table implements the recovery-point pattern pioneered by Brandur Leach (former Stripe engineer):

The recovery_point field tracks progress through multi-step operations (e.g., started → payment_created → stripe_called → finished).

If the process crashes between steps, a retry with the same key resumes from the last committed recovery point18 rather than restarting. The locked_at field prevents concurrent processing of the same key. And a reaper process periodically deletes expired records.

The payment_events table is the immutable audit log:

Every state transition generates an event row.

This table is never updated or deleted. The source field distinguishes whether the transition was triggered by an API call, a webhook, or the reconciliation process, which is invaluable for debugging.

The refunds and ledger_entries tables complete the schema.

Refunds have their own idempotency keys and status lifecycle. Ledger entries follow double-entry bookkeeping: every money movement creates a debit-credit pair grouped by a transaction_id, with a constraint that debits and credits must balance.

Ledger Design

A payment system without a proper ledger is just a database with money in it.

Ledger is the source of truth for every cent that moves through the system…

The core principle is double-entry bookkeeping.

Every transaction produces two entries: a debit on one account and a credit on another. The sum of all debits always equals the sum of all credits. If they don’t match, something is wrong, and you know it immediately.

The ledger table is append-only and immutable.

You never update or delete a ledger record. If a payment is reversed, you don’t modify the original entry. You write a new entry that cancels it. This gives you a complete, auditable history of every state the system has ever been in.

Balance for any account is always computed, never stored directly.

We sum all credits and subtract all debits for that account_id. Storing a balance as a column is tempting for performance, but creates consistency problems: balance can drift from the ledger if a write fails mid-transaction.

Compute it from the ledger and cache it if you need speed.

Reconciliation runs on a schedule, typically at the end of the day, and compares your internal ledger totals against what the payment processor and bank report. Any discrepancy triggers an alert.

At scale, discrepancies occur, and the ledger lets you find and fix them.

Chargebacks and Disputes Lifecycle

A chargeback occurs when a cardholder disputes a charge directly with their bank, bypassing the merchant. The bank reverses the funds immediately, and the merchant has a window (typically 30 days) to contest it with evidence.

From a system design perspective, chargebacks are external events that arrive via webhook from the payment processor. Your system needs to handle them without manual intervention for the common cases.

The lifecycle has distinct states…

Each state transition triggers different actions:

  • notifications to the merchant,

  • freezing related payouts,

  • collecting evidence from the order history,

  • ledger entries to reflect the provisional reversal.

A few design decisions that matter here:

  • Store the full chargeback reason code from the processor.

  • Different reason codes (fraudulent, item not received, duplicate charge) require different evidence and have different win rates.

  • Automating evidence submission for high-confidence cases (clear delivery confirmation, identical billing and shipping address) significantly reduces manual work.

Also track the chargeback rate per merchant. Above 1% is the threshold at which processors start applying penalties, and above 2%, they can terminate the account entirely.

Multi-Currency and FX Handling

Storing money as a float is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes in payments engineering…

Floating point arithmetic introduces rounding errors that compound across millions of transactions. Always store amounts as integers in the smallest currency unit: cents for USD, pence for GBP, yen for JPY (which has no subunit).

The currency code lives alongside every amount in every table.

An amount without a currency is meaningless.

FX handling introduces three specific problems:

  • The exchange rate at the time of charge. When a customer in Germany pays in EUR for a USD-priced product, you need to lock the exchange rate at the moment of the transaction and store it alongside the charge. Exchange rates move. If you look up the rate later for reconciliation, you’ll get a different number and your books won’t balance.

  • Settlement currency. Your payment processor may settle funds in a different currency than the charge. A EUR charge might settle into your USD bank account. The conversion happens at the processor’s rate, which differs from the mid-market rate and from what you showed the customer. So store all three: customer-facing rate, settlement rate, and difference (your FX spread or cost).

  • Rounding rules. Different currencies have different decimal precision. JPY has zero decimal places. KWD has three. Your rounding logic needs to be currency-aware. Always round in the customer’s favor when splitting amounts (for example, splitting a charge across installments). Document your rounding rules and apply them consistently, because auditors will ask.

Marketplace Split Payments

If your platform is a marketplace where customers pay you, and you pay out to sellers or service providers, the standard PaymentIntent flow is not enough.

You need Stripe Connect19.

With Stripe Connect, each seller or service provider has their own connected Stripe account (acct_xxx). When a customer makes a purchase, you create a PaymentIntent with two extra fields: transfer_data.destination (the connected account to pay) and application_fee_amount (your platform’s cut).

Stripe handles splitting the funds automatically at settlement.

The database implications are significant.

The payments table will need a connected_account_id column, as covered in the schema section above. Payouts to sellers become their own state machine: PENDING, IN_TRANSIT, PAID, FAILED.

Refunds on split payments are more complex: if you refund the customer, you must also decide whether to reverse the transfer to the seller, and Stripe treats these as two separate API calls.

The reconciliation process also changes.

You now need to reconcile not just your platform’s balance but each connected account’s balance separately. Stripe’s Connect reporting endpoints (/v1/balance_transactions with the Stripe-Account header) cover this, but it adds a new dimension to your daily reconciliation job.

Idempotency: Most Important Pattern

Idempotency is the foundation on which everything else rests.

At Shopify’s scale, “once in a million” failures happen many times per day. Without idempotency, every network timeout, every retry, every duplicate webhook delivery becomes a potential double charge.

The implementation operates at three layers:

  • At API layer, client generates a UUID v4 idempotency key (typically derived from the cart or session ID) and sends it with the payment request.

    The server’s flow is:

    1. Begin transaction

    2. Attempt to insert the idempotency key (unique constraint rejects duplicates)

    3. If the key already exists, and the recovery point is finished, return the cached response

    4. If the key exists but the recovery point is incomplete, resume from that point

    5. If the key is new, proceed with processing

    6. On completion, update the recovery point to finished and store the response

    7. Commit.

  • At Stripe layer, the same idempotency key (or a deterministic derivative) is forwarded to Stripe via the Idempotency-Key HTTP header.

    • Stripe stores results for 24 hours (API v1) or 30 days (API v2).

    • If you retry with the same key, Stripe returns the cached result.

    • Critically, Stripe compares parameter values: if you send the same key with a different value, it returns an error.

    • The Idempotent-Replayed: true response header identifies a cached response.

  • At webhook layer, Stripe may deliver the same event multiple times.

    • The processed_webhook_events table stores each event’s event_id with a unique constraint.

    • Before processing, the handler checks if the event ID exists. If it does, the event is skipped.

The hardest edge case is a crash between creating the idempotency record and completing the Stripe call…

The atomic phases pattern solves this by breaking the operation into phases separated by foreign state mutations (calls to Stripe, sending emails). Each phase commits its progress to the idempotency key’s recovery_point.

If the process crashes after committing recovery_point = ‘stripe_called’ but before recording Stripe’s response, retry starts from that point.

Since Stripe itself supports idempotency keys, retrying the Stripe call is safe. It returns the original result if the call completed, or processes it if it didn’t.

Retry Strategies and Idempotency

Retrying a payment is not the same as retrying a database query.

If you retry a database query that timed out, the worst case is a duplicate read. If you retry a payment charge that timed out, you may charge the customer twice.

Idempotency keys solve this, too.

Before making any charge request to the payment processor, generate a unique key for that specific charge attempt and include it in the request. If the request times out and you retry with the same key, the processor recognizes it as a duplicate and returns the result of the original attempt rather than processing a new charge.

Most major processors (Stripe, Adyen, Braintree) support idempotency keys natively.

The key should be stable across retries for the same logical operation but unique across different operations. A good pattern is to derive it from the order ID and attempt number: order_789_attempt_1. If the first attempt times out, retry with the same key. If the charge genuinely fails and the user tries again, that’s a new attempt with a new key.

For retry timing, use exponential backoff with jitter20.

Fixed-interval retries create thundering herd problems when a processor has a partial outage: every system retrying at the same interval hits the processor again simultaneously the moment it recovers.

Jitter spreads the retry load.

Define a maximum retry window for each payment type.

A user-initiated charge should retry aggressively for a short window (a few minutes) because the user is waiting. A scheduled subscription charge can retry for hours or days since the consequences of a slight delay are lower.

Exactly-Once Processing Through Optimistic Locking and CAS

Exactly-once processing is achieved through three complementary mechanisms:

  • Optimistic locking uses the version column: every update includes WHERE version = $expected_version and increments the version. If another process updated first, zero rows are affected, and the caller knows it lost the race.

  • Compare-and-swap21 on status combines concurrency control with state machine validation in a single operation:

    This atomically verifies three things: the payment exists, it’s in the correct source state for this transition, and no concurrent update has occurred. If rows_affected = 0, the transition is rejected. This single query is the workhorse of payment state management.

  • The transactional outbox pattern solves the dual-write problem22: you need to update the database and publish an event to a message queue atomically.

    If the database commits but the message fails, you get a lost event. If the message sends but the database rolls back, you get a phantom event. The solution is to write the event to an outbox table within the same database transaction as the payment state change.

    A separate relay process polls for unpublished outbox records (using FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED to handle concurrency), publishes them to Kafka or SQS, and marks them as published.

    This guarantees that every committed state change produces exactly one event.

Data Consistency at System Level

Payments expose a class of consistency problem that most applications never hit: charge can succeed at the payment processor while your own database write fails.

The money has moved, but your system doesn’t know…

This is the dual-write problem:

You’re writing to two systems (the payment processor and your own DB), and there’s no transaction that spans both. If anything fails between those two writes, you end up in an inconsistent state.

The standard solution is to treat the payment processor as the system of record for payment state, and design your own writes to be idempotent and retryable.

The flow looks like this:

  1. Write a pending record to your database before calling the payment processor. This gives you a record that a charge was attempted even if the processor call never returns.

  2. Then call the processor.

    • If it succeeds, update your record to confirmed.

    • If it fails cleanly, update to failed.

    • If it timeout and you don’t know what happened, mark it as unknown and reconcile later.

The unknown state is the hard one.

You need a background reconciliation job that periodically queries the processor for the status of any payment stuck in unknown state and resolves it. This job needs to be idempotent because it will run multiple times against the same records.

The broader principle: never assume a distributed write either fully succeeded or fully failed. Design for the case where you genuinely don’t know, because at scale, that case happens constantly.

Webhook Handling Under Adversarial Conditions

Stripe’s webhook delivery is at-least-once, meaning duplicates are expected.

Webhooks can arrive out of order. A payment_intent.succeeded event might arrive before payment_intent.created because of independent dispatch, network latency, and retry timing.

The system must handle both conditions gracefully.

The webhook receiver’s contract is narrow:

  • verify the HMAC-SHA256 signature using the raw request body (not re-serialized JSON, since any byte-level difference invalidates the signature),

  • check that the timestamp is within 5 minutes to prevent replay attacks,

  • store the raw event, and return 200 immediately.

Stripe retries on any non-2xx response for up to 3 days with exponential backoff across approximately 16 attempts. After 3 days of continuous failures, Stripe disables the endpoint and sends an email notification.

The async processor handles deduplication and ordering:

  • For duplicate events, it checks the processed_webhook_events table. If the event ID exists and is marked processed, skip it.

  • For out-of-order events, the state machine validation acts as the guard: if a payment_intent.succeeded event arrives but the payment is already in CAPTURED state, the transition is either invalid or redundant and is safely rejected.

  • If an event arrives “too early” (the payment doesn’t exist yet), it gets queued for retry with backoff.

  • After exhausting retries (typically 3-5 attempts), failed events land in a dead letter queue23 for manual investigation.

A critical implementation detail: Stripe’s Stripe-Signature header format is t=1614556800,v1=5257a869e7eceb..., where t is the timestamp and v1 is the signature.

Verification requires concatenating {timestamp}.{raw_body}, computing HMAC-SHA256 with the endpoint’s signing secret (whsec_...), and comparing using constant-time string comparison to prevent timing attacks.

Payment State Machine and How to Enforce It

The payment lifecycle is a finite state machine24 with 11 states and strictly defined transitions:

Invalid transitions are the ones that matter most: AUTHORIZED cannot go directly to REFUNDED (must capture first), FAILED cannot go to CAPTURED (terminal state), and CANCELED cannot go to CAPTURED.

These are enforced at three levels:

  • In application code, a VALID_TRANSITIONS dictionary maps each state to its allowed successors, checked before any transition attempt.

  • In the database, the CAS query WHERE status = $expected_status ensures the transition is atomic.

  • Optionally, a PostgreSQL trigger on the payments table can validate transitions as a defense-in-depth measure, though most teams find the application-level check plus the CAS query sufficient.

  • Concurrent state updates are handled by a combination of optimistic locking (via a version column) and the CAS pattern on status. Only one of two concurrent updates will match both the expected status and version, so exactly one succeeds.

Rate Limiting and Abuse Protection

Payment endpoints are high-value targets for two specific abuse patterns that general-purpose rate limiting doesn’t fully address.

Card testing25 is when an attacker uses your checkout flow to validate stolen card numbers.

They submit hundreds of small charges to determine which cards are live before using them elsewhere. The signal is a sudden spike in declined transactions from a single IP or device fingerprint, with amounts clustered around small, round numbers. So rate limit on declines per IP per hour, not just on total requests. A normal user might make 3 payment attempts. 50 declined attempts in an hour from the same IP is card testing.

Velocity abuse26 is when a legitimate-looking account submits an unusually high number of transactions in a short window, either to launder money or to exploit a pricing bug.

Track transaction count and total volume per account per rolling time window. Flag accounts that exceed thresholds for manual review rather than hard-blocking them, since the threshold could also indicate a legitimate high-volume merchant who needs a different account tier.

Beyond these two, standard rate limiting applies:

Limit charge attempts per user session, limit payment method additions per account per day, and limit refund requests per order. Each of these has a corresponding abuse pattern (credential stuffing, card enumeration, refund fraud) that the limit mitigates.

Store your rate limit state in Redis using a sliding-window counter, not a fixed window.

Fixed windows can be gamed by timing requests at the boundary. A sliding window27 counts requests in the true last N seconds regardless of when the window started.

Design for High Availability

Payment systems cannot afford extended downtime.

A 99.999% uptime target means roughly 5 minutes of allowable downtime per year. Reaching that requires thinking about availability at every layer.

The Payment API Service is stateless, so horizontal scaling behind a load balancer handles compute availability trivially.

  • Deploy across at least two availability zones.

  • Use health checks that verify database connectivity, not just process liveness.

The Payment Database is where availability gets hard.

A single leader with synchronous replicas (using Postgres streaming replication or MySQL semi-sync) gives you fast failover with minimal data loss.

But synchronous replication adds write latency since the leader waits for at least one replica to acknowledge. For most payment workloads, this is acceptable, typically under 5ms within a region. For global platforms, distributed SQL databases like CockroachDB or PlanetScale handle multi-region writes with consensus-based replication, at the cost of higher complexity and slightly higher write latency.

The Webhook Receiver must stay up independently of everything else.

If it goes down, Stripe keeps retrying for 3 days, so brief outages are recoverable. Deploy it as a separate service from the Payment API so a spike in webhook volume does not affect checkout latency.

The Message Queue provides natural buffering.

If Background Job Workers go down, events accumulate in the queue and are processed when workers come back up. At-least-once delivery means no events are lost. Size your consumer group so workers can drain the backlog faster than it accumulates.

Circuit breakers28 belong on every external call.

If Stripe’s API degrades, your circuit breaker should trip after a configurable error threshold and stop hammering a struggling service, returning cached responses or graceful errors instead.

Shopify’s open-source Semian library is the production-proven reference implementation for this pattern.

When Stripe Fails or Times Out

The most dangerous scenario in payment processing is a timeout on the Stripe API call.

If the HTTP call to Stripe times out, you don’t know whether the charge went through. The cardholder may have been charged, or they may not have been, and the wrong assumption in either direction is costly.

The correct handling is a three-step protocol:

  • First, record the attempt with status PENDING and the idempotency key used, before making the Stripe call.

  • Second, retry with the same idempotency key. Stripe will return the original result if the request completed, or process it fresh if it didn’t.

  • Third, if retries continue failing, retrieve the PaymentIntent via GET /v1/payment_intents/:id to check its current status, and listen for webhooks as the definitive source of truth.

Stripe’s error types determine the retry strategy.

Network errors (timeout, connection refused), 429 (rate limited), and 500/502/503 (server errors) are retryable, always with the same idempotency key and exponential backoff with jitter. 400 (invalid params) and 402 (card declined) are not retryable.

The card issuer has refused, and retrying will fail again while potentially triggering fraud alerts. The Stripe-Should-Retry response header provides explicit guidance when present.

Shopify’s engineering team recommends aggressive timeout configuration: 1 second open timeout and 5 seconds read/write timeout.

Their circuit breaker library Semian (open source) protects all external calls, including HTTP, MySQL, Redis, and gRPC, with configurable trip thresholds. For payment-specific circuits, they add the merchant’s country code to the circuit breaker identifier, so an outage in one country doesn’t trip circuits globally.

Reconciliation as a Safety Net

Reconciliation is not optional.

It is the critical safety net that catches everything else that can go wrong. Missed webhooks, processing bugs, network failures that left payments in intermediate states, and discrepancies between internal records and Stripe’s records are all caught by reconciliation. Airbnb’s engineering team calls reconciliation “a critical part of payments platforms.”

The daily reconciliation process works as follows:

  1. Pull Stripe settlement data using the Balance Transactions API29 (GET /v1/balance_transactions?payout={po_xxx}) or Stripe’s Reporting API for bulk CSV export, triggered when the payout.reconciliation_completed webhook fires.

  2. Pull internal records for the same period.

  3. Match records on stripe_payment_intent_id.

  4. Compare amounts, currencies, and statuses.

  5. Classify discrepancies: records present in Stripe but missing internally indicate a lost webhook (remediated by re-fetching from Stripe’s API); records present internally but missing in Stripe indicate orphaned records (requiring investigation); amount or status mismatches indicate partial capture/refund issues or state machine bugs.

Internal reconciliation is equally important: comparing payments table against the ledger entries to ensure consistency.

A query checking that every CAPTURED payment has matching ledger entries with the correct amount catches discrepancies between the mutable state table and the immutable audit log.

Refunds Flow Through a Parallel State Machine

A refund follows its own lifecycle:

  1. Merchant initiates a refund via the API,

  2. Backend creates a refund record with its own idempotency key and status PENDING,

  3. Then backend calls Stripe’s refund API (POST /v1/refunds with the charge or PaymentIntent ID and amount),

  4. Transitions the refund to SUCCEEDED or FAILED based on Stripe’s response.

  5. Parent payment transitions to REFUNDED (full) or PARTIALLY_REFUNDED based on the total refunded amount versus the original captured amount.

The Stripe refund itself triggers a charge.refunded webhook.

The backend must handle the case where the webhook arrives before the synchronous API response (due to network timing). The idempotent webhook handler checks whether the refund is already recorded and skips duplicate processing.

Refund settlement follows the reverse flow: funds move from acquiring bank back through the card network to the issuing bank, typically taking 5-10 business days to appear on the cardholder’s statement.

Subscriptions, Saved Payment Methods, and Recurring Billing

When a customer saves their card for future purchases, card details never touch your server.

Stripe handles this through 2 objects: Customer and PaymentMethod.

  • Create a Stripe Customer object on the first payment and store the returned customer_id in your users table.

  • When the customer saves their card, pass setup_future_usage: ‘off_session’ in the PaymentIntent. Stripe attaches the PaymentMethod to the Customer on their side.

  • You store only the Stripe IDs (cus_xxx and pm_xxx), never raw card numbers.

  • For future payments, you pass the stored customer_id and payment_method_id to a new PaymentIntent instead of collecting card details again.

Subscriptions build directly on top of this.

The core objects are a Stripe Product, Price (the billing interval and amount), and a Subscription, which links a Customer to a Price. Your backend stores a subscriptions table with stripe_subscription_id, status mirroring Stripe’s states (trialing, active, past_due, canceled, unpaid), current_period_start, current_period_end, and cancel_at_period_end.

The subscription status in your database is always driven by webhooks, never by your own logic.

Here are the critical webhook events to handle:

  • customer.subscription.updated for plan changes and trial endings,

  • invoice.payment_succeeded to provision access on renewal,

  • invoice.payment_failed to start a grace period,

  • customer.subscription.deleted to revoke access.

When a renewal fails, Stripe’s Smart Retries automatically retries on an optimized schedule over 7-14 days.

During this window, the subscription enters the past_due state. Your application logic must decide what access to allow during the grace period. If retries exhaust without success, PaymentIntent returns requires_action or requires_payment_method, and your background worker must notify the customer to update their card.

Proration30 on plan changes is the trickiest part.

When a customer upgrades mid-cycle, use create_prorations as your proration_behavior setting. This creates an invoice item you can review before charging, rather than billing the difference immediately.

Access Control and Security

Payment systems have a higher security bar than most because the data they hold (card numbers, bank accounts, personal financial information) is directly monetizable if stolen.

On access control, follow least privilege strictly.

The service that processes charges should not have read access to raw card numbers. The service that handles refunds should not have access to initiate new charges. Separate these at the API key and database permission level, not just in application code.

Application-level access control can be bypassed by bugs. Database-level permissions cannot.

Never store raw card numbers.

Use tokenization31: when a card is collected, pass it directly to your payment processor or a PCI-compliant vault, get back a token, and store only the token. The token is useless to an attacker without access to the vault. This also dramatically reduces your PCI-DSS compliance scope, since your systems technically never touch raw card data.

Encrypt sensitive fields at rest beyond what your database provider does by default.

Account numbers, routing numbers, and payout details should be encrypted with a key stored separately from the data. If your database is compromised, encrypted fields without the key are unreadable.

Log every access to sensitive financial data with enough context to reconstruct who accessed what and when. These audit logs are immutable, append-only, and retained for a minimum of 7 years in most jurisdictions. They are not the same as your application logs.

Store them separately with stricter access controls.

Tradeoffs

Here are some tradeoffs every payment backend must make:

1. Synchronous vs async payment processing

The industry consensus is hybrid: synchronous for the customer-facing authorization (the user is waiting), async for everything downstream.

Uber, Airbnb, and Shopify all process the initial charge synchronously for immediate feedback, then handle settlement, reconciliation, retries, and disbursements asynchronously via Kafka. Airbnb explicitly built async patterns for payment methods like Boletos (Brazil), where confirmation takes days.

2. Storing card data vs delegating to Stripe

The universal answer is to never store raw card numbers.

Stripe Elements uses iframes originating from Stripe’s PCI-validated servers, so the PAN never touches the merchant’s infrastructure. This qualifies merchants for SAQ A (~22 requirements) versus SAQ D (~300+ security controls, external auditors, $50K-$500K+ annual compliance costs). Shopify uses iframes with a separate card server to keep its monolith out of PCI scope.

Every major platform, including Uber, Airbnb, and Shopify, uses tokenization.

3. Polling vs webhooks

Stripe’s documentation explicitly recommends webhooks as the primary method and warns against polling due to rate limits.

Webhooks provide near-instant notification; polling adds the polling interval as latency. The production pattern is webhooks for fulfillment, with low-frequency reconciliation polling as a backup safety net. On the frontend, polling the order status after redirect is acceptable for UX, but backend fulfillment should be webhook-driven.

4. SQL vs NoSQL for payment storage

SQL with ACID guarantees wins decisively for core payment data.

Shopify and Uber use MySQL with Vitess. Airbnb uses MySQL.

The reason is straightforward: atomicity ensures a payment either fully completes or fully rolls back; consistency ensures balances are always correct; isolation prevents concurrent transactions from interfering; durability ensures committed transactions survive crashes. Double-entry bookkeeping requires referential integrity.

Regulatory compliance (PCI DSS, SOX) demands auditability and data integrity that SQL provides natively. NoSQL stores serve complementary roles, including Kafka for event streaming and Redis for rate limiting and caching, but never as the primary payment transaction store.

5. Leader-follower vs consensus replication

Most companies start with a leader-follower MySQL or PostgreSQL and add sharding via Vitess or Citus as they scale.

Consensus-based systems (CockroachDB, Google Spanner, TiDB) provide automatic failover with zero data loss and geo-distribution, at the cost of higher write latency from consensus round-trips. SumUp (4M+ merchants across 35 markets) and Shipt use CockroachDB for payment services.

The tradeoff is operational simplicity (leader-follower) vs stronger consistency guarantees (consensus), with the industry trending toward distributed SQL for global payment platforms.

How Shopify, Uber, and Airbnb Do It at Scale

Shopify built its own payment product, Shopify Payments, on top of Stripe’s infrastructure, and handles extraordinary scale: 173 billion requests on Black Friday 2024, peaking at 284 million requests per minute.

Their payments architecture uses MySQL with Vitess for sharding, a poded multi-tenant architecture (extra-large merchants get dedicated pods), and their open-source circuit breaker library Semian. Their published “10 Tips for Building Resilient Payment Systems” emphasizes low timeouts (1s open, 5s read/write), idempotency keys for every payment and refund, correlation IDs flowing from controller through background jobs to SQL query comments, and load testing starting 5 months before Black Friday. Payment states are modeled as state machines.

Once a payment is resolved or rejected, the state is final.

Uber rebuilt their payment platform around four principles: high reliability through active-active architecture, exactly-once processing through idempotency and strong consistency, auditability through double-entry bookkeeping, and scalability to new business lines and geographies.

Their architecture processes thousands of transactions per second across two main subsystems: Collections (money in from riders/eaters) and Disbursements (money out to drivers/restaurants). They consolidated 70 different payment endpoints into a Unified Checkout orchestration layer, yielding a 3% increase in conversion rate and 4.5% improvement in session recovery, translating to hundreds of millions in additional annual revenue.

For rides, they use two-phase payments: authorize when the ride starts, capture when it ends.

Airbnb evolved from PayPal with paper checks to a sophisticated domain-driven payment platform processing transactions across 191 countries in 75+ currencies.

They use a multi-PSP strategy: Adyen handles the majority of global card processing, Stripe powers bank account payments, and PayPal is offered as an option for buyers who prefer it. Their current architecture decomposes into capability-oriented services: Pay-in, Payout, Processing, Ledger, and Settlement and Reconciliation. They built Orpheus, a custom idempotency framework that divides every major workflow into a DAG of retryable idempotent steps.

Their data model is append-only: modifications are modeled as new versions in a sequence, providing a complete history. To support 20+ local payment methods across 14 countries, they built three standardized payment flow archetypes (redirect, async, direct) with a connector- and plugin-based architecture for onboarding new PSPs.

Closing Thoughts

The main principle of payment backend design is that correctness is achieved through layers of defense, not any single mechanism.

Idempotency keys prevent duplicate charges. The state machine prevents invalid transitions. Optimistic locking prevents concurrent corruption. The outbox pattern prevents lost events. Reconciliation catches everything the other layers miss.

The architecture should be synchronous on the customer-facing path and asynchronous for everything else. SQL databases with ACID guarantees are non-negotiable for core payment data. Webhooks are the source of truth, but only when processed idempotently and backed by reconciliation. And every external call, whether to Stripe, card networks, or partner services, must be treated as unreliable, with the system designed to recover gracefully from any failure at any point.

The most sophisticated insight from studying production payment systems at Shopify, Uber, and Airbnb is this: exactly-once processing is not a property of any single component. It is an emergent property of at-least-once delivery, combined with idempotent processing and reconciliation.

Each layer is simple. The correctness comes from their composition.

PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) The set of security requirements every company that touches card data must comply with. Compliance is tiered: SAQ A (~22 requirements) applies when card data never touches your servers (Stripe Elements handles collection). SAQ D (~300+ controls, external auditors, $50K-$500K+ annual cost) applies when you handle card data directly. Using a PSP like Stripe and keeping card data off your servers keeps you in SAQ A.

2

Level 1 = You are responsible for securing card data at scale.

3

PaymentIntent A stateful object Stripe uses to track a single payment from start to finish. Moves through states: requires_payment_method, requires_action, processing, and succeeded. Replaces older “charge” APIs by handling modern complexity like 3D Secure challenges and fraud checks within a single lifecycle object.

4

3D Secure (3DS) An additional authentication layer for card payments where the cardholder verifies the transaction with their bank, typically via a one-time code or biometric in their banking app. Triggered when the PaymentIntent returns requires_action. Shifts fraud liability from the merchant to the issuing bank when used.

5

ISO 8583 authorization message is the standardized message format used by card networks (Visa, Mastercard, etc.) to communicate payment transactions, especially during authorization. Think of it as the “language” banks and payment networks use to talk to each other.

6

  • Issuing bank = the customer’s bank (the one that gave them the card)

  • Acquiring bank = the merchant’s bank (the one that receives the money)

7

BIN (Bank Identification Number) The first 6-8 digits of a card number. Identifies the issuing bank and card network. The acquiring bank uses the BIN to determine which card network (Visa, Mastercard) to route the authorization request to.

8

AVS (Address Verification System) A fraud check that compares the billing address submitted by the cardholder against the address on file with the issuing bank. One of several checks the issuing bank runs during authorization, alongside CVV verification and fraud risk models.

9

Capture The step where the merchant instructs the acquiring bank to finalize an authorized amount and initiate the actual money movement. Can happen immediately (digital goods) or be delayed until fulfillment (physical goods, hotels, ride-hailing). You can capture less than the authorized amount but never more.

10

Authorization Hold A temporary hold placed on the cardholder’s available balance when a payment is authorized. No money moves at this point. The hold is released either when the merchant captures the payment or when it expires, typically after 5-10 days depending on the card network.

11

Interchange Fee The largest component of card processing fees, paid to the issuing bank (the cardholder’s bank). On a typical US online transaction, interchange accounts for roughly $2.05 of the 2.9% + $0.30 Stripe fee. Covers the issuing bank’s risk and funds the cardholder’s rewards program.

12

Two Generals Problem A theoretical problem in distributed computing proving that two parties communicating over an unreliable channel can never achieve guaranteed consensus. In payments, this means true exactly-once delivery is mathematically impossible. The practical solution is at-least-once delivery combined with idempotent processing and reconciliation, which together produce the same observable result as exactly-once.

13

Idempotency Key A unique identifier attached to a payment request that allows safe retries without risk of duplicate charges. If the same key is submitted twice, the processor returns the result of the original request rather than processing a new charge. Stripe stores results for 24 hours (API v1) or 30 days (API v2). If parameters differ on a retry with the same key, Stripe returns an error.

14

HMAC-SHA256 A cryptographic algorithm used by Stripe to sign webhook payloads. The receiver recomputes the signature using the raw request body and the endpoint’s signing secret, then compares using constant-time string comparison to prevent timing attacks. Verifying this signature before processing any webhook is mandatory.

15

Transactional Outbox Pattern A pattern that solves the dual-write problem between a database and a message queue. Instead of publishing directly to the queue, events are written to an outbox table within the same database transaction as the state change. A separate relay process polls for unpublished outbox records and publishes them to the queue. Guarantees every committed state change produces exactly one event.

16

Double-Entry Bookkeeping An accounting principle where every money movement is recorded as two entries: a debit on one account and a credit on another. The sum of all debits always equals the sum of all credits. If they don’t match, something is wrong. The ledger is append-only: mistakes are corrected by writing new reversing entries, never by updating or deleting existing ones.

17

Optimistic Locking A concurrency control technique using a version column. Every update includes WHERE version = $expected_version and increments the version on success. If another process updated first, zero rows are affected and the caller knows it lost the race and must retry.

18

Recovery Point A field on the idempotency key record that tracks progress through a multi-step operation. If the process crashes mid-operation, a retry with the same key resumes from the last committed recovery point rather than restarting from scratch. Pioneered by Brandur Leach, former Stripe engineer.

19

Stripe Connect Stripe's platform product for marketplace and multi-party payments. Each seller or service provider has their own connected Stripe account (acct_xxx). Payments are created with a destination account and an application fee, and Stripe handles splitting funds at settlement. Required for any platform where money flows through to third-party recipients.

20

Exponential Backoff with Jitter A retry timing strategy where each retry waits progressively longer (exponential) with a random offset added (jitter). Prevents thundering herd problems where all retrying systems hit a recovering service simultaneously at fixed intervals. Jitter spreads the retry load across time.

21

Compare-and-Swap (CAS) An atomic operation that updates a record only if its current state matches an expected value. In payments, used as WHERE status = $expected_status AND version = $expected_version to atomically validate a state transition and prevent concurrent updates in a single query.

22

Dual-Write Problem The consistency challenge that arises when writing to two separate systems (a payment processor and your own database) with no transaction spanning both. If anything fails between the two writes, the systems are left in inconsistent states. Solved by writing a pending record before calling the processor, and using a reconciliation job to resolve any payments left in unknown state.

23

Dead Letter Queue (DLQ) A queue that receives messages that have failed processing after exhausting all retries. In webhook processing, events that cannot be successfully processed after 3-5 attempts are moved to the DLQ for manual investigation rather than being silently dropped.

24

Finite State Machine A model where a system can be in exactly one of a defined set of states at any time, with strictly defined valid transitions between states. Payment lifecycles are modeled as state machines to prevent invalid transitions (for example, AUTHORIZED cannot go directly to REFUNDED, FAILED cannot go to CAPTURED). Enforced at the application layer, database layer via CAS queries, and optionally at the database trigger level.

25

Card Testing A fraud pattern where attackers use a checkout flow to validate stolen card numbers by submitting many small charges to determine which cards are live. Detected by rate limiting on declined transactions per IP per hour rather than total requests, since normal users rarely have more than a few declines.

26

Velocity Abuse A fraud pattern where an account submits an unusually high volume of transactions in a short window to launder money or exploit pricing bugs. Mitigated by tracking transaction count and total volume per account per rolling time window and flagging rather than hard-blocking accounts that exceed thresholds.

27

Sliding Window Counter A rate limiting algorithm that counts requests within a true rolling time window rather than fixed intervals. Prevents gaming of fixed-window rate limits by timing requests at window boundaries. Implemented in Redis for sub-millisecond performance at scale.

28

Circuit Breaker A pattern that stops sending requests to a failing external service after a configurable error threshold is reached, returning cached responses or graceful errors instead. Prevents a struggling downstream service from being overwhelmed by continued requests. Shopify’s open-source Semian library is the production reference implementation for this pattern.

29

Balance Transactions API Stripe’s API endpoint for retrieving settlement data. Used in daily reconciliation to pull Stripe’s record of all money movements for a given payout period and compare against internal records. Discrepancies where records exist in Stripe but not internally indicate lost webhooks.

30

Proration The calculation of a partial billing amount when a subscription plan changes mid-cycle. When a customer upgrades, the unused portion of the current period is credited and the new plan’s cost for the remaining period is charged. Using create_prorations as the proration_behavior setting creates a reviewable invoice item rather than immediately billing.

31

Tokenization Replacing sensitive card data with a non-sensitive token. When a card is collected via Stripe Elements, the raw card number (PAN) is sent directly to Stripe’s PCI-validated servers, which return a token (pm_xxx). Your system stores only the token. The token is useless to an attacker without access to Stripe’s vault, and dramatically reduces your PCI-DSS compliance scope.
