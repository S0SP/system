October 2014 - Prague, Czech Republic.

Maria has an important meeting in 15 minutes.

But she doesn’t have cash for a taxi.

So she opens the ride-sharing app called Uber & requests a ride.

Although it’s stunning how easily Uber processes payments, handling payments at ‘scale’ is extremely HARD.

Here are some problems:

1. Security

A user adds payment details on the mobile app.

Yet a mobile device could get stolen, or Uber servers could get breached…

So it’s highly risky to store sensitive information on mobile devices & Uber servers.

2. Disbursement

A single payment has to be split across different trip participants:

  • Driver

  • Uber platform

  • Tax, fees, and so on

i.e., money cannot simply move directly from rider to driver.

3. Reliability

Payments include “external systems” such as banks, card networks, and payment providers.

But there’s a risk of network failures, timeouts, and partial outages.

And this could create a dreadful user experience.

Payment System Design

Here’s how Uber payment architecture works:

1. Add Payment Information

A user adds payment details, such as credit card number & CVV, to the mobile app.

Yet it’s RISKY for them to store this data because:

  • It’s hard to secure

  • It requires heavy legal compliance

So they use payment provider1 SDK2 in the mobile app to collect sensitive details.

Software development kit (SDK) doesn’t send card information to Uber servers.

Instead, it “securely” collects credit card information and sends it directly to the payment provider, along with contextual metadata. Metadata includes Uber account details, payment method type (card or wallet), and so on.

Payment provider then returns a unique token,,, which acts as a ‘safe reference’ for the credit card. The token represents the card; think of it as a (reusable) permission to charge the card.

Plus, the payment provider binds the received metadata to the token to scope its usage and validation.

From this point on, mobile app uses the token instead of the credit card.

i.e., Uber or mobile device has NO sensitive data.

IMPORTANT NOTE:

A stolen token is useless because it’s created by the payment provider, tied to Uber’s account and app, and restricted to specific uses (such as charges made only by Uber). So another mobile device or app cannot use it to charge the card.

2. Ride Request

Here’s what happens when the user requests a ride:

  1. Mobile app makes a request to Uber’s backend.

  2. Payment service performs ‘risk’ checks3, such as checking stolen card databases, velocity checks4, and so on.

  3. Afterwards, Payment service authenticates itself to the Payment provider using secret credentials.

  4. Payment service sends the TOKEN to the Payment provider and requests a “pre-authorization” for the estimated fare.

  5. Payment provider checks whether the token is valid & rejects it if not.

  1. Payment Provider forwards the request to the Bank via the Card network (Visa/Mastercard).

  2. Bank approves or declines, and Payment provider reports the results.

  3. Then Bank places a TEMPORARY HOLD on the funds, i.e., no money moves; instead, simply reserves funds.

  4. User sees a pending charge on their bank account.

  5. Temporary money hold remains in place throughout the ride… If the ride is cancelled, the payment service asks the payment provider to release the hold.

Plus, Payment provider returns a ‘transaction ID’ to Uber’s backend.

It’s used for payment status checks and cancellations later.

3. Trip Completion

Here’s what happens when a trip is complete:

  1. Trip service computes the FINAL FARE… and sends a ‘business event’ to the Payment service.

  1. Payment service creates a payment order5 with a unique ID6 and persists it in the Payments database (Payment Orders table7) with state8 = NOT_STARTED9.

  2. Afterwards, Payment service publishes the payment order event to Apache Kafka10.

  3. Order processor consumes the event… and prepares the payment execution using the computed fare (driver earnings, platform fees, tax).

  4. After that, Order processor publishes a durable payment “Intent”11 to Kafka to record the decision to charge the rider.

  5. An Order processor worker then picks up the “Intent” and calls Payment provider to charge the rider.

  1. Plus, it asks Payment service to update the payment order table: state = EXECUTING12.

  2. Payment provider returns the result via a synchronous API response or an “asynchronous” webhook13,,, and Order processor writes it to Kafka.

  3. Order processor takes the result from Kafka and asks Payment service to update the payment order table: state = SUCCESS or FAILED accordingly.

NOTE: Payment orders table updates its status ONLY when all trip participants for the same transaction get processed successfully.

4. Payment Distribution

Payment provider converts the authorization hold into a real charge.

If:

  • final fare < hold → release the extra hold

  • final fare > hold → request extra authorization

NOTE: Money from the rider’s account isn’t directly transferred to the driver’s account.

Here’s the high-level workflow for money movement:

  1. Uber calculates platform fees, driver earnings, taxes, payment network fees, and so on.

  2. Money moves from the rider’s bank account to Uber’s bank or platform account after trip completion.

  3. Uber moves money to the driver’s bank account by calling the Payment provider.

Besides, they update the "ledger and wallet” after a successful payment.

Now, let’s look into databases for tracking payments…

Ledger:

  • Immutable ‘double-entry’ bookkeeping system.

  • The sum of all transactions must be 0.

  • Think of it as a financial record of a transaction; it tracks the debit to the rider, the credit to the driver & platform.

  • A custom database built on top of NoSQL databases.

  • It answers:

    • What is the financial truth?

    • Who owes what?

    • Can we audit this?

Wallet:

  • Aggregated balance views derived from the ledger.

  • It stores the current balance (driver earnings & platform balance).

  • Also, it shows how much a rider has paid.

  • It answers:

    • How much money is available right now?

    • Can we pay this out?

Payments Database

  • It tracks payment orders… and their execution state.

  • It answers:

    • Did the payment occur?

    • Did it succeed or fail?

    • Is it safe to retry?

Yet it’s risky to combine these databases, as it could make auditing unreliable and make partial failures hard to fix. So Uber’s payment system keeps them separate.

Some Useful Information

  • Encrypt data to prevent data tampering in transit.

  • Use tokenization to protect credit card information.

  • Use HTTPS (TLS) to prevent eavesdropping on traffic.

  • Replicate databases & backup data to prevent data loss.

  • Rate limit requests & use firewalls to prevent DDoS attacks14.

  • Use TLS and certificate pinning15 to avoid man-in-the-middle attacks16.

  • Verify Payment provider’s webhooks to prevent fake payment status updates.

There are at least 2x transactions per trip because of many participants. With around 15 million trips per day, Uber’s total number of transactions is significantly higher.

1

A payment provider is a service that securely processes payments by connecting an app or site to card networks and banks. It lets the business charge customers without handling sensitive card data themselves. Payment providers used by Uber are Adyen, Stripe, and Braintree. It’s invisible to the rider.

2

A payment provider SDK is a ready-made piece of software that an app or site could plug into to collect payment details without handling sensitive data itself.

3

Payment SDK only checks that the card exists and can be tokenized, while risk checks decide whether this specific payment looks fraudulent or abusive before charging it.

4

Velocity checks are fraud checks that look for unusually fast or repeated actions, such as many payments or ride requests in a short time, to detect suspicious behavior.

5

Payment Order is money movement information along with metadata. It includes:

  • Amount

  • Idempotency Key

  • Currency

  • Rider ID

  • Trip ID

6

A unique ID is necessary so the payment service can safely retry failed requests, track the payment across systems, and prevent double-charging the rider if the same payment is processed more than once.

7

Payment Orders table lives in a strongly consistent OLTP (SQL) database - Uber Payments database. This database is the source of truth for:

  • where a rider was charged,

  • whether a retry is allowed,

  • whether a charge succeeded or failed.

Here’s the table schema for PaymentOrders:

  • order_id,

  • trip_id,

  • amount,

  • status,

  • idempotency_key,

  • txn_id,

  • timestamp

8

Payment Orders table has an enumerated data type (ENUM) to track the execution status of a payment order.

9

The state is NOT_STARTED because the system has only recorded the intent to charge, but has not yet called the payment provider.

10

Apache Kafka is a system that lets services reliably send, store, and process streams of events, enabling asynchronous operation and independent scaling.

11

There’s a risk of network failure or late responses when calling the payment provider. Publishing “Intent” to Kafka makes it easy to retry, scale, and recover. Hence, the “Intent” pattern ensures payment execution reliability and prevents double payments.

12

EXECUTING means the payment service has initiated the charge by calling Payment provider and is awaiting the final result.

13

Webhook is a URL registered with the payment provider during its initial setup. Payment Service updates its database with the information received from the webhook.

14

A DDoS attack is when attackers flood a system with massive traffic to overwhelm it and make it unavailable to users.

15

Certificate pinning is a security technique where an app trusts only a specific server certificate (or public key) and rejects all others, even if they are valid, to prevent man-in-the-middle attacks.

16

A man-in-the-middle attack is when an attacker secretly intercepts and possibly alters communication between two parties who think they are talking directly to each other.
