Every large language model1 (LLM) you use has lied to you with confidence, fluency, and frequency…

Ask any model about something that happened last week. It doesn’t know. It can’t know. Its knowledge was frozen months ago. They might try, and if they do, they will hallucinate.

This isn’t a bug.

It’s a fundamental architectural limitation. LLMs keep knowledge in their parameters2. These are billions of numerical weights learned during training. Once training ends, the knowledge is locked. The model doesn’t know what it doesn’t know, so it fills the gaps with confident fabrication. Studies show that hallucination3 rates can be as low as 1% for simple summarization tasks. However, they can exceed 58% for complex work.

This is exactly what Retrieval-Augmented Generation4 (RAG) solves.

RAG doesn’t bake knowledge into the model. Instead, it pulls in relevant context when you ask a question. The model stays smart, your data stays current, and every answer is traceable back to its source.

This is the architectural pattern that makes AI actually useful.

The Knowledge Problem

We need to understand why LLMs struggle with real-world knowledge before we fix them. The problem has three layers, and none of the obvious solutions work…

LLM knowledge is frozen at training time

Every LLM has a cutoff date for its knowledge.

Everything the model knows was scraped, processed, and compressed into parameters during training. After that date, the model is blind. It isn’t aware of your new product launch, yesterday’s security issue, or this morning’s HR policy change.

This isn’t a minor inconvenience.

In business, accuracy is crucial. This includes areas like customer support, legal analysis, internal search, and compliance. A model that can’t access current information is a liability. It doesn’t help the business at all.

Context Windows Aren’t the Answer

The brute-force approach is tempting: dump everything into the prompt.

Context windows5 have increased a lot. Some models can now handle over a million tokens6. But this approach has three fatal problems.

First, it’s expensive.

You pay for each token. Sending your entire knowledge base with every query will wipe out your budget.

Second, there are hard limits.

A million-token window can’t fit all of a large enterprise’s documents or its databases.

Third, and this is the one most people miss, models get worse with more context.

Research from Stanford and UC Berkeley shows LLM performance follows a U-shaped curve. Models do best when key information is at the start or end, but accuracy drops sharply when important facts are buried in the middle. In Liu et al.’s multi-document QA experiments, accuracy for some models dropped to roughly 25% when key information was placed in the middle of a 20-document context.

It’s clear: adding more context to the prompt doesn’t guarantee the model will use it.

Fine-tuning isn’t the answer either

Fine-tuning7 is when you take a pre-trained model and continue training it on your own specific data, so it learns new knowledge or behavior.

Fine-tuning changes the model’s weights to incorporate new knowledge. Think of it like sending someone back to school for a specialized course. In theory, this lets you teach the model about your domain.

In practice, it creates more problems than it solves…

It requires GPU compute, machine learning expertise, and carefully prepared training data. It takes days or weeks to complete. The result is a snapshot. Once your underlying data changes, your fine-tuned model becomes stale.

What’s Actually Needed

The key is to provide the AI with the right information at the right time for each query. And it should be done without changing the model itself.

That’s RAG.

Now let’s investigate RAG deeper:

What RAG actually is

Retrieval-Augmented Generation is an architectural pattern, not a product. The concept is straightforward, and the best analogy is an open-book exam.

A student taking a closed-book exam relies on what they have memorized.

That’s a standard LLM. It’s smart and can reason, but it’s limited to what’s in its parameters. A student with an open-book exam has the same reasoning skills. They can check relevant pages before answering.

That’s RAG.

The formal definition comes from a 2020 paper by Patrick Lewis and his team at Facebook AI Research and University College London. RAG models combine parametric memory8 with a pre-trained language model and non-parametric memory9. This non-parametric memory uses an external knowledge index. It’s accessed by a neural retriever10.

You can still update the model’s knowledge by swapping the retrieval index. No one needs to retrain.

In practice, RAG follows a three-step loop:

  1. Query comes in: a user asks a question.

  2. Retrieve: System looks in an external knowledge base for the best information chunks.

  3. Question and context go to the LLM. It then creates an answer based on the retrieved documents.

You’re not changing the model. You’re changing what it sees. That distinction makes RAG so powerful and so practical.

The open-book analogy makes sense at a high level. But understanding why it works so well means diving deeper into the topic…

How RAG Works Under the Hood

The three-step loop sounds simple. The engineering that makes it work is where things get interesting.

A RAG system has two main parts:

  • An offline ingestion pipeline that gets your data ready.

  • An online retrieval pipeline that answers queries.

Let’s walk through each one…

Embeddings: Core Mechanism

Before anything else, you need to understand embeddings11. This is the concept that enables semantic search12.

Text embeddings turn text into dense numerical vectors13. These are arrays of floating-point numbers, usually with 1,536 or 3,072 dimensions. They capture the text meaning. The magic is in what “capture meaning” means. Words and sentences with similar intent are close together in vector space. This happens even with different words.

Consider this: “How do I reset my password?” and “I can’t log into my account” use completely different words.

But when converted to embeddings, they produce nearly identical vectors. The distance between them is tiny because their meanings are similar. This is measured using cosine similarity14, dot product, or Euclidean distance.

The key insight for RAG is clear.

System finds relevant content. It does this even if the user’s question doesn’t use the exact words from the source document. It searches by meaning, not by keywords.

Data Ingestion Pipeline (Offline Phase)

First, process your knowledge base. Then, index it. Only then can your RAG system answer questions.

This happens in five steps:

What triggers the offline phase?

In traditional workflows, this pipeline runs when new data is available. This can happen when documents are added or updated. It can also occur when a database changes or on a regular schedule, like nightly or weekly. Some teams trigger re-ingestion if retrieval quality drops.

They also do this when a new data source connects:

  1. Load documents from anywhere: PDFs, databases, APIs, wikis, Slack channels, and Confluence pages. Frameworks like LangChain15 and LlamaIndex16 offer ready-made connectors for many common sources.

  2. Chunking17: Split documents into semantically meaningful pieces. This is the single highest-leverage step to get right, and we will cover it in depth later.

  3. Embedding: Convert each chunk into a vector using an embedding model.

  4. Storage: Store the vectors in a vector database18 optimized for similarity search.

  5. Metadata tagging: Tag each chunk with source, timestamp, category, and access control information. This metadata becomes critical for filtering, attribution, and security later.

Retrieval Pipeline (Online Phase)

When a user asks a question, the retrieval pipeline kicks in:

  1. Query embedding: User’s question turns into a vector. This uses the same embedding model from ingestion. This is critical: the query and the documents must live in the same vector space.

  2. Similarity search: The system finds the top-K19 similar chunks. It compares the query vector to each vector in the database.

  3. Retrieval strategy: This is where the real engineering decisions happen. Three primary approaches exist:

    1. Sparse retrieval20 uses a statistical method that matches exact keywords. It weighs these matches using term frequency and inverse document frequency.

    2. Dense retrieval (embeddings): Semantic search via vectors. Finds conceptually relevant content even when the wording differs.

    3. Hybrid search (combining both): Use both sparse and dense retrieval. Then, merge the results with Reciprocal Rank Fusion21 (RRF). This boosts documents that rank high in both systems.

  4. Re-ranking: After retrieving results, a cross-encoder model rescans them. It processes the query and each document as one input. This captures fine-grained relevance that bi-encoders miss.

Generation Phase

With the most relevant chunks in hand, the system assembles the final prompt:

  1. Prompt Construction:

    1. System Prompt: Combine the system prompt (the instructions that tell the LLM how to behave, like “You are a helpful customer support agent”) with the user’s request.

    2. Retrieved Context Chunks: Integrate the relevant pieces of text pulled from your knowledge base. For instance, if someone asks, “What’s your refund policy?” You might pull two paragraphs from your company’s policy document.

    3. User’s Original Query: Include the user’s actual question or task — exactly as they typed it.

  2. LLM Call: The model generates an answer grounded in the retrieved documents. The key facts are in the prompt. The model uses these facts to reason instead of relying on its trained memory. Think of it like giving someone an open-book exam instead of asking them to answer from memory.

  3. Citation and Attribution: The system shows which source documents were used and provides verifiable citations. For example, the response might say, “Based on Section 3.2 of the Employee Handbook...” so the user knows exactly where the answer came from. This is one of RAG’s biggest advantages over fine-tuning: transparency.

Now that you know how RAG works mechanically, the real question is whether it’s the right tool. That depends on what you’re comparing it to…

Why RAG over alternatives?

RAG isn’t the only approach to giving AI access to knowledge.

You need to know when to use it and when not to. This means comparing it to other options.

RAG vs. Fine-Tuning

This is the comparison most teams face first. Here’s how they stack up:

The key nuance: fine-tuning and RAG are complementary, not competing.

Fine-tuning is the best option when you want to adjust the model’s behavior, style, or output format, not just its knowledge. The best production systems do two things: they fine-tune style and behavior, and then they use RAG for knowledge. You can adjust a model to produce structured JSON in a certain format.

Then, use RAG to fill it with up-to-date data.

RAG vs. Long Context Windows

Context windows keep growing. Why not dump everything in the prompt?

Cost and precision. RAG retrieves only the 5-20 most relevant chunks. Long context stuffs everything in and hopes the model finds the needle. RAG is cheaper per query. It’s also more precise in what it finds. Plus, it can search millions of documents, unlike others that have a token limit.

They work well together: first, use RAG to get the right 20 chunks.

Then, use long context to process them all at once. The “lost in the middle” research shows that retrieving small, relevant chunks works better than using large context windows.

Traditional RAG vs. Agentic RAG

Traditional RAG follows the straightforward three-step loop described above: retrieve, then generate. It works well for simple question-answering but has limitations. What happens when the first retrieval doesn’t return good results? Traditional RAG just pushes forward with whatever it finds.

Agentic RAG adds a reasoning layer on top.

An AI agent decides how to handle each query. It can change the search, link several retrievals, pick a knowledge base, or skip retrieval completely. Think of Traditional RAG as a student who looks up one page and writes their answer. Agentic RAG is like a student. They check several sources. They re-read sections that seem off and cross-reference information before writing.

Multi-Step RAG Pipeline

Production RAG systems often go beyond the simple retrieve-and-generate loop. A multi-step pipeline adds intelligence before and after retrieval:

  1. Query Intent Parsing: Before searching, the system analyzes what the user actually wants. Is it a factual question? A comparison? A request for a summary? Understanding intent helps the system choose the right retrieval strategy and knowledge base.

  2. Query Reformulation: The system may rewrite the user’s question to improve retrieval quality. For example, “Why is my app slow?” might become “application performance bottleneck causes and solutions.”

  3. Retrieval: System searches for relevant chunks (as described above).

  4. Live Web Search: If internal documents aren’t enough, some RAG systems can do live web searches. This helps them get current information from the internet.

  5. Reranking22 and Filtering: Results are scored, filtered, and reranked for relevance.

  6. Generation: LLM produces an answer grounded in all the gathered context.

This multi-step approach is what separates demo-quality RAG from production-quality RAG.

RAG Limitations

RAG is powerful, but it’s not a silver bullet…

Understanding its limitations helps you decide when to use it and when to look elsewhere:

  • Static retrieval: Traditional RAG retrieves documents once per query. If the first retrieval misses, the answer suffers. (Agentic RAG addresses this, but adds complexity.)

  • Retrieval quality bottleneck: RAG is only as good as what it retrieves. If your chunking is poor, your embeddings won’t be strong. Also, if your data is poorly organized, the LLM will get bad context. This leads to bad answers. Garbage in, garbage out.

  • No reasoning over missing data: If the answer isn’t in your knowledge base, RAG can’t help. The model may still hallucinate or simply say it doesn’t know. RAG doesn’t create knowledge; it surfaces existing knowledge.

  • Not good for action execution: RAG is a retrieval pattern, not an action pattern. It can guide you on what to do using documentation. But it can’t do things like update a database, send an email, or deploy code. For that, you need tool-use patterns like MCP (covered below).

Knowing why RAG wins is one thing. Knowing how to build it right is another thing.

This is where most teams run into trouble…

Building RAG in Production: What Actually Matters

Getting a RAG demo working takes a weekend. Getting a RAG system into production takes engineering discipline.

Here are the problems that will bite you, and how to solve them:

1 Chunking Strategy

Problem: Bad chunking equals irrelevant retrieval equals wrong answers. This is the single highest-leverage optimization in your entire pipeline.

Approach: Start with 400-512 token chunks and 10-20% overlap. In a 2024 NVIDIA benchmark, researchers tested 7 chunking strategies on 5 datasets. Page-level chunking had the highest accuracy at 0.648. It also had the lowest standard deviation. Testing on FinanceBench showed that a 15% overlap worked best with 1,024-token chunks.

Key strategies to consider:

  • Fixed-size / token-based: Simple, predictable.

  • Semantic chunking: Groups text by meaning using embeddings. Higher quality, higher compute cost.

  • Page-level chunking: Uses document structure. It won the NVIDIA benchmark for consistency across document types.

  • Document-aware chunking: Respects headers, sections, and tables. Best for structured documents.

Why does overlap matter? If a key sentence is split into two parts, overlap makes sure both parts include the full idea.

2 Security and Access Control

Problem: If your RAG system has many users or roles, some users may see documents they shouldn’t access. Security is a major barrier to enterprise AI adoption. Surveys show that most organizations see security concerns as their main obstacle.

Approach: Enforce permissions at the retrieval layer. Tag each chunk with access-control metadata during ingestion. Filter at query time based on the user’s role.

There are two patterns:

  • Pre-filtering: This checks document permissions before retrieval.

  • Post-filtering: This checks document permissions after retrieval. Then, it sends them to the LLM.

Pre-filtering is best for large datasets with low hit rates. Post-filtering is effective when most documents are available.

Never rely on the LLM to “not mention” restricted content. The model has NO concept of authorization. If a restricted context lands in the prompt, it will use it.

Enterprise deployments require Fine-Grained Authorization (FGA) in the RAG pipeline. They must also comply with frameworks like GDPR, HIPAA, and SOC 2.

3 Observability and Debugging

Problem: When RAG gives a bad answer, you don’t know where things broke. Was it a problem with ingestion (bad chunks), retrieval (wrong chunks returned), or generation (the model missed good chunks)?

Approach: Log at each step.

  • Query

  • Retrieved chunks with similarity scores

  • Assembled prompt

  • Final response

4 Caching

Problem: Every query runs embedding, vector search, and an LLM call. At high traffic, this gets expensive and slow.

Approach: Semantic caching23. If a new query is similar to a recent one, return the cached response. Amazon Bedrock’s prompt caching cuts costs by up to 90% for repeated input tokens.

5 Cost at Scale

Problem: RAG isn’t free, and costs compound. Costs typically skew heavily toward LLM inference, with vector database operations and compute making up the remainder.

Approach: Use cost optimization strategies:

  • Prompt caching: Up to 90% cost reduction on cached input tokens for repetitive prompts.

  • Batch processing: 50% savings versus on-demand pricing.

  • Hybrid Retrieval Routing: Route keyword-heavy queries to BM2524. This can cut costs by over 50% on those queries.

  • Open-source embeddings: BGE-M3 eliminates embedding API costs entirely.

Separation of Memory Types

Understanding how LLMs use different types of memory helps clarify where RAG fits:

  • Parametric Memory: Knowledge stored in the model’s weights during training. This is “baked in” and doesn’t change after training. It’s how the model knows Paris is the capital of France.

  • Non-Parametric Memory: External knowledge model access at query time through retrieval. This is the document store, database, or index that RAG searches. It can be updated at any time without retraining the model.

  • Short-Term/Context Memory: The information in the current prompt or conversation. This is the context window, what the model can “see” right now.

RAG connects parametric and non-parametric memory. It allows the model to use up-to-date knowledge from outside sources. This way, it also keeps its trained reasoning skills.

RAG + MCP: Two Pillars of Production AI

If you read our newsletter on Model Context Protocol, you’ll see how these two patterns meet…

RAG solves the knowledge retrieval problem: “What should the AI know?”

MCP solves the tool and data connectivity problem: “What should the AI do?”

RAG is fundamentally a retrieval mechanism for unstructured, static data. MCP enables action execution and real-time access to structured data.

Together, they form two pillars of production AI architecture. The combo is strong: an AI agent gets context using RAG and makes decisions with MCP tools.

Concretely, you can build an MCP server that exposes a RAG pipeline as a tool:

Any MCP-compatible AI client, like Claude Desktop or Cursor, can query your knowledge base. This is done via the standard MCP protocol. This pattern has several existing implementations. The architecture flows from the agent to the MCP client, then to the MCP server, and finally to the vector database.

Research is already pushing this further…

A 2025 paper on RAG-MCP discusses “prompt bloat.” This issue happens when there are too many MCP servers. Their solution uses retrieval to pick the most relevant MCP server for each query. Red Hat released an implementation guide in January 2026. It focuses on building AI agents that use MCP and RAG integration patterns.

Here’s where it all connects: standardized connectivity (MCP) and grounded knowledge (RAG). Together, they make AI agents that can know things and do things.

Closing Thoughts

RAG isn’t a product.

It’s an architectural pattern. Now, it’s the main way to give AI access to private, real-time, domain-specific knowledge. RAG has become one of the most widely adopted patterns in enterprise AI. The market is expected to grow to $10 billion by 2030.

The pattern works because it respects a fundamental separation of concerns.

The model handles reasoning. The retrieval system handles knowledge. You can upgrade either independently. Swap models without rebuilding your data pipeline. Update your knowledge base without retraining anything.

For backend engineers, RAG is one of the highest-leverage AI skills to learn right now.

It doesn’t require ML expertise. It requires the skills you already have -- data pipelines, APIs, databases, and system design. The concepts are familiar. The tools are mature. The demand is enormous.

1

LLM (Large Language Model): A type of AI that has been trained on massive amounts of text data to understand and generate human language. Examples include ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. Think of it as a very advanced autocomplete that can write paragraphs, answer questions, and reason through problems.

2

Parameters: The internal numerical values (weights) that a model learns during training. They encode everything the model “knows.” A model with 70 billion parameters has 70 billion of these learned numbers that together determine how it responds.

3

Hallucination: When an AI generates information that sounds confident and correct but is actually made up. It’s not “lying” on purpose. It’s pattern-matching from training data and filling gaps when it doesn’t have the real answer.

4

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation): An architecture where an AI retrieves relevant documents from an external source before generating an answer, so it can reference real, up-to-date information instead of relying solely on its training data.

5

Context Window: The maximum amount of text a model can “see” at once, including both the input prompt and the output it generates. Think of it like the model’s working memory; it can only hold so much at a time.

6

Token: A unit of text that LLMs process. Roughly, 1 token is about 3/4 of a word in English. So 1,000 tokens is about 750 words. When people say a model has a “128K context window,” they mean it can process about 96,000 words at once.

7

Fine-Tuning: The process of taking a pre-trained model and continuing its training on a smaller, specialized dataset. This adjusts the model’s weights so it performs better on specific tasks or adopts a particular style. It’s like giving a general-purpose employee specialized training for a specific role.

8

Parametric Memory: Knowledge stored inside the model’s weights. It’s “baked in” during training. The model doesn’t look anything up; it generates answers from these stored patterns. Like a person recalling facts from memory.

9

Non-Parametric Memory: External knowledge that the model looks up at query time rather than storing internally. In RAG, this is your document store or vector database. Like a person checking a reference book instead of relying on memory.

10

Neural Retriever: A small neural network trained specifically to find relevant documents given a query. It converts both the query and documents into vectors and finds the closest matches.

11

Embeddings: Numerical representations of text that capture meaning. Each piece of text becomes a list of numbers (a vector), where similar meanings produce similar number patterns. This lets computers measure how related two pieces of text are, even if they use completely different words.

12

Semantic Search: Searching by meaning rather than by exact keywords. Instead of matching the words you typed, it understands what you meant and finds content with a similar meaning. For example, searching “affordable housing” would also find results about “low-cost apartments.”

13

Vector: An ordered list of numbers. In AI, a vector might contain hundreds or thousands of numbers that, together, represent the meaning of a word, sentence, or document. You can think of it as coordinates in a very high-dimensional space, where similar meanings are close together.

14

Cosine Similarity: A mathematical way to measure how similar two vectors are. It looks at the angle between them. A score of 1 means identical direction (very similar meaning), 0 means no relationship, and -1 means opposite. It’s the most common way RAG systems measure relevance.

15

LangChain: A popular open-source framework for building applications with LLMs. It provides pre-built components for common tasks such as connecting to data sources, managing prompts, and chaining multiple LLM calls.

16

LlamaIndex: An open-source framework specifically designed for building RAG applications. It specializes in data ingestion, indexing, and retrieval, making it easier to connect LLMs to your data.

17

Chunking: The process of breaking large documents into smaller, manageable pieces before storing them. Think of it like cutting a textbook into individual sections; each chunk should contain a complete thought or idea, so the model gets useful context when it retrieves one.

18

Vector Database: A specialized database built to store and search vectors (embeddings) efficiently. Unlike traditional databases that search by exact values, vector databases find the most similar vectors using mathematical distance.

19

Top-K: The number of most-similar results returned by a search. If K=5, the system returns the 5 chunks most relevant to your query. Tuning K is a trade-off: too few, and you might miss important context; too many, and you include irrelevant noise.

20

Sparse Retrieval: A search method that matches based on exact keyword overlap, using algorithms like BM25 or TF-IDF. Called “sparse” because the document representations are mostly zeros (sparse vectors). Fast and good at exact matches, but misses synonyms and related concepts.

21

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF): A technique for combining results from multiple search systems. It gives higher scores to documents that rank well across both systems. For example, if a document ranks #2 in keyword search and #3 in semantic search, RRF combines these ranks into a single score.

22

Reranking: A second-pass scoring step after initial retrieval. A more powerful (but slower) model re-examines each retrieved document alongside the original query to produce more accurate relevance scores. Think of it as a quality-check round that re-sorts results for better accuracy.

23

Semantic Caching: Storing previous query-response pairs and reusing them when a similar question is asked later. Unlike exact-match caching, it uses embeddings to recognize that “What’s the return policy?” and “How do I return an item?” are essentially the same question.

24

BM25: A classic keyword-based search algorithm. It scores documents by how often query terms appear (term frequency) weighted by how rare those terms are across all documents (inverse document frequency). Still widely used and surprisingly effective for keyword-heavy queries.
